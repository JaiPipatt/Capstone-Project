\section*{\pending{Abstract}}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

Modular self-reconfigurable robots (MSRRs) offer a single set of hardware that
changes its own body plan to suit whatever terrain it meets. That property is
what makes them attractive for planetary surface exploration and for search and
rescue: launch mass and volume are constrained, the environment cannot be
mapped in advance, and a robot stopped by a crevasse or a crater-rim ledge is a
robot that has ended its mission. Two decades of research have largely settled
the \emph{shape-oriented} problem --- given a target configuration, compute and
execute a reconfiguration sequence. Deployment requires the inverse,
\emph{task-oriented} framing: perceive the environment, infer the capability
the obstacle demands, and derive the configuration. The closest existing
demonstrations of that loop still depend on external motion capture, off-board
computation, or a single dedicated sensor module, and learning-based
reconfiguration remains almost entirely confined to simulation --- precisely
where contact dynamics and magnetic docking fail to transfer.

This project designs, builds and evaluates a number of mechanically homogeneous
MSRR modules together with a physics-validated digital twin, and closes the
perceive~$\rightarrow$~select~$\rightarrow$~reconfigure~$\rightarrow$~traverse
loop using onboard sensing and computation only. The module reverse-engineers
the SMORES architecture: a four-degree-of-freedom hybrid chain/lattice/mobile
body whose two side rotations serve interchangeably as differential-drive
wheels and as articulated joints, fitted with genderless electropermanent
(EP-Face) connectors that hold without standby power. Positioning is
cascaded --- ultra-wideband ranging fused with inertial measurement for
macro-scale relative localisation, infrared sensing and IMU feedback for
short-range alignment --- and perception is deliberately low-SWaP, pairing a
monocular camera with an $8\times8$ multi-zone time-of-flight sensor rather
than an RGB-D camera. Reconfiguration planning inherits the SMORES-EP
Kuhn--Munkres task-assignment and parallel self-assembly pipeline, extended
with a prioritised multi-robot route planner and, above it, the
configuration-selection layer that makes the system task-oriented rather than
shape-oriented. Development is staged across four phases, from external
perception and computation with contact-free locomotion, through docked
reconfiguration, to fully onboard perception and computation. Success is
measured against three obstacle classes --- gap, step and narrow passage ---
with targets of at least 80\,\% configuration-selection success in simulation
including held-out obstacle parameters, at least 90\,\% autonomous dock/undock
over twenty hardware trials, and closure of the full autonomous loop on one
obstacle class in hardware.

\medskip
\pending{\noindent\textbf{Keywords.}\quad self-assembling robot; modular robot;
hierarchical control; onboard perception; SMORES-EP; autonomous docking; search
and space exploration.}

\pending{\gsection{Introduction}}

\pending{%
A planetary rover is built once, launched once, and then meets terrain nobody
has surveyed at the scale that matters to it. Orbital imagery resolves features
of tens of metres; the things that end missions are smaller than that. A
crevasse a little wider than the wheelbase, a crater-rim ledge a little taller
than the wheel radius, the mouth of a lava tube narrower than the chassis ---
each is invisible from orbit and impassable on arrival. The conventional answer
is to drive around, and where driving around is impossible the mission stops
there. \emph{Spirit} ended its operational life in soft regolith it could not
reverse out of; the same class of problem, at the same scale, recurs wherever a
fixed-shape vehicle meets unsurveyed ground.

The constraint behind this is not intelligence but geometry. A wheeled rover has
one body plan, chosen years before launch against terrain models that are
necessarily coarse. Whatever that plan cannot cross, it cannot cross. Sending
several specialised vehicles instead multiplies launch mass and volume, the two
resources a planetary mission has least of, and each vehicle remains a single
point of failure for its own speciality.

Modular self-reconfigurable robots (MSRRs) attack the constraint directly. An
MSRR is a set of identical modules, each able to drive on its own and to dock
rigidly to its neighbours, so that the collective can change its own body plan
in the field. Four properties make the architecture a good fit for space and
for search and rescue specifically, rather than merely an interesting machine:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Shape change substitutes for route planning.} The same modules
        drive as independent wheeled units on open ground, chain into a bridge
        to span a gap, or form a cantilever arch to climb a ledge. Terrain that
        defeats one morphology is crossed by another.
  \item \textbf{Launch mass and volume are amortised.} One set of homogeneous
        modules covers the capabilities that would otherwise require several
        purpose-built vehicles, and identical modules pack more densely than
        articulated ones.
  \item \textbf{Redundancy is intrinsic.} Modules are interchangeable, so a
        failed unit is replaced from the remaining pool rather than ending the
        mission --- the property demonstrated in self-repair after
        catastrophic damage \cite{yim2007ckbot}.
  \item \textbf{Access scales below the vehicle envelope.} A single module can
        enter an opening the assembled robot cannot, then reassemble on the far
        side. Confined voids --- lava tubes, collapsed structures, ducts ---
        are reachable in a way no fixed chassis reaches them.
\end{itemize}

Two decades of research have made the mechanism work. Connectors, module
geometries and reconfiguration algorithms are well catalogued
\cite{liang2025,dai2024}, and platforms exist that dock autonomously and
reconfigure on demand. What remains open is the decision, not the motion.
Almost all of this work is \emph{shape-oriented}: the target configuration is
supplied from outside the system, and the robot computes how to reach it. On a
planetary surface there is nobody outside the system to supply it. Round-trip
light time to Mars runs from roughly six minutes to over forty, so a rover
facing an unexpected obstacle cannot ask; it must decide.

That inversion --- from ``given this shape, reconfigure'' to ``given this
obstacle, choose a shape'' --- is the problem this project addresses. It
requires three things the literature has not yet combined: perception of the
obstacle from the robot's own sensors, inference of the capability the obstacle
demands, and selection of a configuration that supplies it, all with
computation carried on board. The nearest existing system closes a version of
this loop but depends on a dedicated sensor module and off-board computation
\cite{daudelin2018}, and the learning-based approaches to configuration
selection are almost entirely confined to simulation, where contact dynamics
and magnetic docking are precisely what fails to transfer.

This proposal sets out the design, construction and evaluation of an MSRR that
closes that loop end-to-end on real hardware. Section~\ref{sec:background}
establishes the technology trends, the societal need and the resources
available, and states the problem precisely. Section~\ref{sec:objectives} fixes
the objectives and the criteria they will be measured against. The remaining
sections cover the literature reviewed, the concept and methodology, the
project timeline, and the theoretical background the starting period requires.
}

\gsection{Background and Problem Statement}\label{sec:background}

\noindent\textit{Current technology trends, societal needs, and the available
resources.}

\gsubsection{Current technology trends}

Modular self-reconfigurable robotics (MSRR) has matured from an academic
concept into a technology actively pursued for space and search-and-rescue
missions. Lattice, chain and mobile architectures are well documented
\cite{stoy2010,yim2007grand}, and a recent survey of mechanisms and design
catalogues the available connectors and module geometries \cite{liang2025}.
Demonstrated platforms span precision-docked chains (SMORES and SMORES-EP
\cite{davey2012,liu2023}), freeform magnetic systems (FreeBOT
\cite{liang2020}) and outdoor tracked swarms \cite{zhao2024}. The 2026
\textit{Science Robotics} review frames the field's direction as on-demand
multifunctional application rather than further mechanism proliferation
\cite{liang2026}.

The hybrid-joint module --- where a module's degrees of freedom serve
interchangeably as driving wheels or as articulated bending joints --- was
established by the SMORES platform, whose cubic modules carry four degrees of
freedom, two of which act either as differential-drive wheels on flat terrain
or as rotational joints once docked \cite{davey2012}. Electropermanent
magnetic docking, which holds without continuous power draw, belongs to the
later SMORES-EP work \cite{liu2023}, not to the original 2012 design. The
paradigm has since moved out of the laboratory: a snail-inspired modular swarm
using a hybrid connector has been shown collectively adapting to unstructured
outdoor terrain \cite{zhao2024}, evidence that terrain-adaptive reconfiguration
is an open problem rather than a solved decade-old one.

In parallel, modular reconfigurable hardware is being pursued for space because
of its redundancy, compact launch volume, and adaptability to unknown terrain.
This is not conceptual only: decentralised reinforcement learning is being
developed for modular reconfigurable lunar robots \cite{mishra2025}, and
goal-oriented path planning has been demonstrated for modular
self-reconfigurable satellites \cite{liub2025}.

\medskip
\noindent Software and onboard sensing are the bottleneck, not mechanism
design:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Most demonstrations depend on external motion capture, overhead
        cameras, or off-board compute. Self-contained relative localisation has
        only recently been treated as a problem in its own right
        \cite{liuy2025,tu2025}.
  \item Perception pipelines assume a fixed sensor pose; a reconfiguring body
        breaks that assumption.
  \item Learning-based reconfiguration is almost entirely simulation-only
        \cite{wu2023,pathak2019,yuan2022,dobreva2025}. Contact dynamics and
        magnetic docking are precisely what fails to transfer.
\end{itemize}

\gsubsection{Societal and industrial needs}

\noindent\textbf{Space and planetary surface operations.} Reconfigurable
modules reduce launch mass and volume relative to shipping several
single-purpose robots, and provide redundancy, self-servicing and repair where
human intervention is impractical \cite{mishra2025,liub2025}. A conventional
wheeled rover crosses a flat plain easily but is stopped by a crevasse, a
crater-rim ledge, or a confined lava-tube void --- the terrain that shape
change exists to defeat.

\noindent\textbf{Search and rescue.} Unstructured, non-mappable environments
where morphology change substitutes for routing around obstacles
\cite{stoy2010,zhao2024}. Urban search and rescue remains the standard proving
ground for adaptive all-terrain mobility.

\noindent\textbf{Inspection of hazardous or inaccessible infrastructure.} Small
openings leading to large interior volumes --- pipes, ducts, damaged structures
--- where shape change is the only way in.

\noindent\textbf{Manufacturing.} Reconfiguration to match the current product
on a line, suited to small-batch production.
\note{Optional --- cut if Section~\ref{sec:background} runs long}

\gsubsection{Problem statement}

\pending{%
A space search mission is specified before launch; the environment it meets is
not. The object of a search is known as a description rather than a location,
so neither the route to it nor the obstacle that blocks that route can be
enumerated in advance, and orbital survey resolves terrain far coarser than the
features that actually stop a vehicle. Launch mass then forbids the obvious
response: a specialised vehicle per contingency is exactly what the budget
cannot carry, and with no resupply each such vehicle is a single point of
failure for its own speciality.

An MSRR answers both from one property --- the modules are identical. One
homogeneous set covers capabilities that would otherwise need several
purpose-built vehicles, and any module substitutes for any other, so a failed
unit is drawn from the same pool that supplies the capability range
\cite{yim2007ckbot}. Mass spent on redundancy is not mass withheld from
capability. That holds only if the robot can choose its own shape in the field.
}

\gsubsection{Available resources}

\noindent\textbf{Prior art to build on, not reinvent.} Two decades of MSRR
research supply reusable building blocks. On hardware: proven connector designs
and complete module architectures (SMORES \cite{davey2012}, SMORES-EP
\cite{liu2023}, FreeBOT \cite{liang2020}) with published strength, cycle-time
and power figures. On software: goal-oriented reinforcement learning
\cite{liub2025}, hierarchical mechanochemical control \cite{meng2011}, and
library-driven mission planning \cite{jing2018} provide a validated starting
point. Foundational literature is already collected: the Stoy et al.\ textbook
\cite{stoy2010} and the Yim et al.\ grand-challenges survey
\cite{yim2007grand}.

\noindent\textbf{Project resources.} Four members; 200,000\,THB; FDM printing,
machining, electromagnetic field lab. Team background: ROS\,2, SLAM,
computer vision.

\gsubsection{Scope and limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Negotiate obstacles from three defined classes using onboard perception
        and compute only. No movement scripts, no prior map, no external
        localisation.
  \item Prototype-scale terrestrial testbed as a planetary analogue. No claim
        of space-flight qualification.
  \item Fixed module count in hardware, the count itself being an output of
        actuator sizing rather than an input. Scaling behaviour beyond that
        count is addressed in simulation only.
  \item Single defined working environment, to be specified with dimensions,
        surface and lighting.
\end{itemize}

\gsubsection{Main objective}

Design, build and evaluate a number of MSRR modules and their physics-validated
digital twin, such that the system perceives an obstacle, selects a
configuration from a learned policy, reconfigures, and traverses --- using
onboard sensing and compute exclusively.

\pending{%
\noindent\textbf{Calibration of the hardware targets.} These figures are set
against the closest comparable end-to-end result on this class of hardware.
Daudelin et al.\ \cite{daudelin2018} categorise 24 recorded failures of one
SMORES-EP demonstration: 10 (41.7\,\%) hardware, 6 (25\,\%) perception,
4 (16.7\,\%) human error, 3 (12.5\,\%) navigation, 1 (4.2\,\%) network.
Hardware reliability, not algorithm quality, was the largest single failure
cause on a mature research platform. Note that this is a breakdown of failure
causes, not a success rate --- the paper does not report one, and the figures
should not be quoted as a proportion of attempts. Two consequences are built
into the criteria above. First, connector reliability is measured separately
from the full loop, so a hardware limitation is visible as such rather than
absorbed into an end-to-end number. Second, the post-navigation docking target
sits deliberately below the pre-aligned target, since the reference system's
experience indicates that the additional stages carry most of the risk.

\noindent\textbf{Reporting convention.} Criteria are stated as trial counts
rather than percentages. At ten or twenty trials the confidence interval around
a success rate is wide, and reporting ``7 of 10'' rather than ``70\,\%'' keeps
the sample size visible to the reader.
}

\gsection{Literature Survey and Review}\label{sec:litreview}

\gsubsection{Locomotion mechanism design}

Reviewed SMORES and SMORES-EP for the four-DoF layout and the use of the
connector disks as differential-drive wheels \cite{davey2012,liu2023}; PolyBot
for open-loop, table-driven chain gaits \cite{yim2003}; Sambot for a mobile
module with a separately rotating docking face and a self-locking worm drive
\cite{wei2011}; and CEBOT, SMC, Millibot and Swarm-bots as mobile systems that
lose chain locomotion once docked \cite{wei2011}.

\gsubsection{Communication system (data transmission)}

Reviewed SMORES (XBee) and SMORES-EP (Wi-Fi to a central computer, with pose
from external motion capture) for wireless approaches \cite{davey2012,liu2023};
PolyBot (CAN) and M-TRAN (electrical contacts) for buses routed through the
connector \cite{yim2003,murata2002}; CONRO and CKBot for per-face infrared
\cite{castano2002,yim2007ckbot}; Sambot for a two-medium scheme switched at
docking \cite{wei2011}; and the EP-Face inductive UART channel
\cite{tosun2016}.

\gsubsection{Low-level control and sensing}

Reviewed SMORES for the single-MCU architecture, motor drivers and
potentiometer joint sensing \cite{davey2012}; Sambot for a master-and-slave
controller arrangement with encoders, gyroscope and accelerometer
\cite{wei2011}; PolyBot G3 for Hall-effect joint sensing \cite{yim2003}; and
CONRO for distributed control running on an 8-bit microcontroller
\cite{castano2002}.

\gsubsection{Reconfiguration planning}

Reviewed Liu et al.\ \cite{liu2023} for the SMORES-EP self-assembly framework
--- root-module search, Kuhn--Munkres task assignment, and depth-wise parallel
action groups --- adopted here as the planner's backbone since it targets the
same hardware; Liu and Yim \cite{liu2017} for the connection-graph notation
used to describe a configuration (\textsc{Face}, \textsc{Face2Con},
\textsc{Orientation}); Binder et al.\ \cite{binder2019} and Binder
\cite{binder2017} for prioritised multi-robot route planning with
spatio-temporal vertex locking, collision-resolution strategies and
precondition-based execution, named by Liu et al.\ as the planner for their
navigation stage and adopted here; Daudelin et al.\ \cite{daudelin2018} for the
only end-to-end demonstration of perception-driven reconfiguration on this
hardware --- environment classification, library-based configuration selection,
autonomous reconfiguration and task execution --- which supplies the system
template, the localisation arrangement adopted for Phase~A (RGB-D SLAM for the
environment map, AprilTag fiducials read by a downward-facing camera for module
pose during reconfiguration), and --- in the authors' own stated limitation of
``oversimplified environment classification'' --- the gap this project targets;
Jing et al.\ \cite{jing2016,jing2018} for the property-indexed design library
that selection mechanism rests on, in which each entry pairs a configuration
and behaviour with the environment properties it suits (stair height, tunnel
height, gap width, ground roughness); and Liu et al.\ \cite{liu2019} for
distributed reconfiguration planning, not adopted because its per-module
autonomy is unnecessary at a fleet size of 4--6 and adds coordination overhead
our centralised staging does not require.

\gsubsection{Simulation and assembled locomotion}

Reviewed Gazebo \cite{koenig2004} and MuJoCo \cite{todorov2012} as candidate
simulators --- the former for its ROS\,2 integration and sensor models, the
latter for its contact model and for equality constraints that can be toggled
at runtime, which map directly onto docking and undocking; MJX, MuJoCo's
accelerator-backed branch, for reinforcement-learning throughput; ROS\,2
\cite{macenski2022} as the integration layer holding the control loop; PPO
\cite{schulman2017} as the policy-gradient method for contact-rich gaits; Tobin
et al.\ \cite{tobin2017} and Peng et al.\ \cite{peng2018} for domain
randomisation as the standard route to sim-to-real transfer, the latter for
dynamics rather than visual randomisation; Whitman et al.\ \cite{whitman2023}
for modular-robot control policies conditioned on assembled topology, reviewed
but not adopted because our configuration count is small enough to treat each
assembled configuration as a distinct robot and train one policy per
configuration, rather than one network generalising across topologies;
PolyBot's open-loop table-driven gaits \cite{yim2003} together with the
hand-authored behaviour library of Jing et al.\ \cite{jing2016,jing2018}, in
which each configuration carries a manually designed behaviour, as the
established state of practice for assembled-configuration locomotion and
therefore the baseline against which learned policies are measured; and
Daudelin et al.\ \cite{daudelin2018} for the fragility of open-loop behaviours
under environmental variation, which the authors of that system identify as one
of its stated limitations and which motivates learning closed-loop policies for
contact-rich configurations.

\gsubsection{Positioning and alignment system}

Reviewed UWB--INS-based relative positioning for robust relative localisation
of unmanned ground vehicles, using UWB measurements with an error-state
iterated extended Kalman filter (ES-IEKF) to estimate relative position
\cite{zhang2022}; Sambot for its two-stage infrared docking approach, using
passive IR sensing for approach and active IR sensing for final docking
alignment together with IMU feedback \cite{wei2011}; and SMMRob for modular
self-reconfiguration and coordinated localisation among modules
\cite{li2022}. These approaches motivate a hierarchical positioning and
alignment system for SMORES-EP, in which UWB provides coarse relative
positioning over larger distances, while IR sensing and the IMU are used for
short-range alignment and docking.

\gsubsection{Docking interface}

Before committing to a connector, we weighed nine designs across FreeBOT
\cite{liang2020}, CAST \cite{khoshnevis2003}, the snail-inspired hybrid
connector \cite{zhao2024}, SMORES-EP \cite{liu2023}, Roombots
\cite{sprowitz2009}, M3Express \cite{wolfe2012}, the self-aligning EPM
interface \cite{wangstokes2025}, TYCOON \cite{tycoon2026}, and the UBot
multi-sensory docking approach \cite{zhu2014}. FreeBOT, the snail connector,
and UBot were dropped early: FreeBOT's point-magnet attraction is too weak in
tension to support a lifted cantilever, the snail connector is mechanically
complex and impractical to implement within our design constraints, and UBot's
staged vision/Hall-sensor approach solves a perception problem we are already
solving ourselves rather than a connector problem.

CAST and the self-aligning EPM interface were set aside for the opposite
reason. They are compliance-driven designs that reduce the precision our
navigation controller must hit, but neither has been demonstrated on a
self-mobile module with the wiring, sensing, and structural loads our task
requires. M3Express and Roombots were dropped on power and load grounds
respectively: M3Express's off-board control model conflicts with our
onboard-perception requirement, and Roombots' motor-actuated latch is sized for
furniture loads well beyond our torque budget.

This leaves TYCOON and SMORES-EP as the two strongest candidates. TYCOON is
particularly promising for the lifting problem, as its complementary
intermittent gear mechanism allows one module to reinforce a neighbouring
joint's torque without requiring a second motor, potentially reducing the
cantilever risk identified in chain configurations. However, SMORES-EP's
genderless docking interface provides greater freedom of movement while
enabling fast, low-power state changes, and its EP-Face has a published
characterisation of holding force and acceptance area \cite{tosun2016}. We
therefore select SMORES-EP's electropermanent connector as the baseline, while
treating TYCOON's torque-sharing mechanism as a possible future addition to the
joint drivetrain rather than a replacement for the docking interface.

\gsubsection{Task-oriented planning}

The reconfiguration planning most relevant to this project is the SMORES-EP
line of work from the ModLab group. Liu, Whitzer and Yim \cite{liu2019} present
a distributed reconfiguration planner that transforms an initial configuration
into a target configuration while minimising disconnect and reconnect
operations. Liu, Lin, Kim and Yim \cite{liu2023} extend this into a parallel
self-assembly framework using Kuhn--Munkres task assignment and depth-based
grouping of docking actions. Both approaches assume that the target
configuration is already given, making them suitable as the shape-oriented
baseline for our reconfiguration system, which we intend to leave unchanged.

Daudelin et al.\ \cite{daudelin2018} instead address the
configuration-selection problem by choosing from a library of pre-verified
configurations based on task and environmental requirements, while treating the
actual reconfiguration as a separate process. Other studies explore
configuration selection through graph-based methods, including
centre-configuration selection \cite{liujg2007}, maximum-similarity subchain
matching \cite{liuh2026}, subconfiguration decomposition \cite{wei2023}, and
graph-isomorphism-based assignment \cite{dutta2019}. Learning-based approaches
have also been explored, including joint configuration and motor-primitive
selection \cite{kojcev2018}, multi-agent reinforcement learning for freeform
reconfiguration \cite{wu2023}, and selecting between existing reconfiguration
algorithms \cite{buchi2025}.

Existing work shows that modular robot configurations are primarily represented
as graphs or bounded sets of predefined configurations, rather than being
generated directly from raw environmental data. The literature also suggests
that using a limited set of pre-verified configurations provides a more
practical starting point than unrestricted configuration search.

\gsubsection{Built-in camera for perception}

Reviewed SMMRob for onboard visual perception in compact self-assembling
modular robots, demonstrating that visual sensing can enhance perception and
support relative positioning and docking \cite{li2022}; terrain traversability
analysis using RGB-D vision and terrain probing to evaluate geometric, semantic
and physical properties of unknown environments \cite{haddeler2022};
lightweight multi-zone time-of-flight depth sensing with an $8\times8$ sensor
and low-computational-cost decision making for obstacle avoidance on
resource-constrained robots \cite{muller2023}; real-time robot vision on
low-performance computing hardware using lightweight visual processing methods
\cite{lan2018}; and low-cost monocular vision for road detection and following
in mobile robots \cite{zhang2018}. These studies motivate a lightweight onboard
perception system for SMORES-EP that combines a monocular camera for visual
terrain perception with compact ToF sensing for local depth information. The
resulting terrain and traversability information can then be used by the leader
module to support transformation selection, rather than relying solely on
conventional object detection.

\gsubsection{Compute module}

Reviewed SMORES-EP for onboard processing used alongside external motion
capture \cite{liu2023}; CKBot for a dedicated camera module carried one per
cluster with processing on the camera \cite{yim2007ckbot}; and the end-to-end
task system for AprilTag localisation running on an external laptop
\cite{jing2018}.

\gsection{Project Concept Development}\label{sec:concept}

\noindent\textit{Theoretical background, technical depth, innovation, and
practical relevance.}

\medskip
The system is developed in four phases. Each phase keeps the mechanical
platform of the one before it and moves one dependency from outside the robot
to inside it, so that a failure at any stage is localised to the dependency
just internalised rather than to the platform as a whole.

\begin{longtable}{@{}L{0.08\textwidth} L{0.86\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Phase} & \textbf{Definition} \\
\midrule
\endhead
A & External perception and computation. Basic locomotion of the individual
    module with three-DoF onboard odometry and localisation, following the
    SMORES architecture, with no inter-module contact --- locomotion only.
    Communication is a manually configured star topology to the commander. The
    system is shape-oriented at this stage. \emph{Goal: reverse-engineer the
    SMORES platform.} \\
B & External perception and computation. The same module fitted with the
    SMORES-EP electropermanent connector, now with contact --- chain, lattice
    and mobile reconfiguration. The system becomes task-oriented. \\
C & Internal perception, external computation. The same SMORES-EP module, with
    sensors mounted on the leader module and sensor data carried over a wired
    link to an external processing unit. \\
D & Internal perception and internal computation on the same SMORES-EP module
    --- the full onboard target. \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

%------------------------------------------------------------------- Phase A --
\gsubsection{Phase A --- Locomotion, sensing and communication}

\gsubsub{Locomotion mechanism design}

Building a hybrid modular robot that combines chain, lattice and mobile
reconfiguration gives two distinct problems. First, the module must drive
itself on the ground to a docking site or to another node. Second, once
assembled it must act as a joint. Speed drives the first, torque drives the
second, and both run through the same gear train. SMORES is the only published
platform that does all three reconfiguration styles in one module
\cite{davey2012,liu2023}, so we will reverse-engineer and mimic that
architecture first, then enhance the holding torque at the joints to allow more
connected modules and a wider range of assembled actions.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \begin{subfigure}[b]{0.46\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/image2.png}
    \caption{Module layout and gear train.}
  \end{subfigure}\hfill
  \begin{subfigure}[b]{0.40\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/image1.png}
    \caption{Degrees of freedom.}
  \end{subfigure}
  \caption{The SMORES four-DoF hybrid module architecture adopted as the
           Phase~A baseline \cite{davey2012}.}
  \label{fig:smores-module}
\end{figure}

The module inherits the SMORES-EP four-DoF layout: left, right and pan rotate
continuously, and tilt has a limited angular range. The two side rotations
double as wheels for differential drive, and the same two faces serve as
docking faces. SMORES produces its four DoF with four 298:1 gear motors, each
driving a 9-tooth pinion into a 48-tooth spur gear for a further 5.33:1
\cite{davey2012}. Left and right each have their own motor, so they can be
commanded independently for steering. Pan and tilt come from the other two
motors through a 48-tooth crown gear coupled to both inner spur gears:
counter-rotating them pans, co-rotating them tilts. Only the tilt case combines
both motors' torque, which makes tilt the strongest DoF --- useful in the chain
configuration, where tilt carries the cantilever load.

\gsubsub{Low-level control and sensing}

For low-level control, a microcontroller on every module handles joint control,
sensor sampling and the data link. The original SMORES ran five motors,
including the latch motor, from a single mbed LPC1768 (Cortex-M3, 96\,MHz)
\cite{davey2012}. We plan to use either an STM32 or an ESP32 family part, each
for a different reason. The STM32 is built for actuation control and has CAN
support on chip, which keeps a wired inter-module protocol open as a future
option. The ESP32 has Wi-Fi and Bluetooth built in, so no additional wireless
module is needed. The two differ in core architecture --- the STM32G4 is an ARM
Cortex-M4, while the ESP32 family uses Xtensa or RISC-V cores --- so the choice
also fixes the toolchain.

For joint position the original SMORES used continuous-rotation potentiometers
with 5$^\circ$ resolution \cite{davey2012}; we will use a Hall-effect or
magnetic encoder instead, to reduce the error carried into joint odometry. Each
joint runs a closed-loop position controller on the module's own MCU from
encoder feedback, driving the motors through dual H-bridge drivers --- SMORES
used three TB6612FNG parts for five motors \cite{davey2012}. Current sensing
gives torque feedback and detects stall or contact, which is also how the
module confirms it has reached a docking face. An IMU is added for attitude and
orientation relative to gravity; SMORES lists this as future work, and Sambot
already carries one (ADXRS613 gyroscope, ADXL335 accelerometer)
\cite{wei2011}.

The module also needs internal room for the EP contact system, the IR and UWB
alignment sensors, and the connection to the compute module carrying the
perception system.

\gsubsub{Communication system (data transmission)}

The link must work in two conditions --- when modules are on their own, and
when they are connected as one structure --- and it should not change medium at
docking.

The original SMORES used an XBee radio so that ``the docking connections need
only worry about mechanical attachment'' \cite{davey2012}. SMORES-EP moved to
Wi-Fi with an onboard processor on every module at about 40\,Hz, but still to a
central computer, and takes module pose from an external VICON system at
100\,Hz \cite{liu2023}. We plan to remove both external dependencies.

We will use Wi-Fi. For the ESP32 it is already on chip, so no extra radio is
needed. A radio keeps communication independent of the docking mechanism ---
the same reason SMORES used XBee. PolyBot and M-TRAN instead route a bus
through the connector \cite{yim2003,murata2002}, which adds a contact
reliability failure at every joint and constrains the connector design. One
radio also covers both conditions with no handover, unlike Sambot, which
switches from ZigBee to CAN once docked \cite{wei2011}.

SMORES-EP reports a second option we may use later. Connected EP-faces can
exchange data through the magnetic coupling of the mated EP-magnets, and the
paper states the faces are capable of UART serial communication
\cite{tosun2016}. This is contactless, so it avoids the contact reliability
problem of a wired bus, but the paper only reports the capability and does not
characterise it, so there is no measured rate or error figure to design
against. It is also unlikely to be a high-bandwidth channel, since the magnets
are driven by 3\,ms pulses. Its real value is that the channel is face-local,
so a message identifies which face it arrived on. We treat it as a possible
addition for topology discovery, not as a replacement for the radio, so the
baseline never depends on a medium change at docking. For the STM32G4, its
built-in CAN stays available as a wired option on the same terms, once the EP
contact system can carry data.

The link carries the data needed to run the cluster: joint angles from each
DoF, IMU attitude, connector state on each face, battery state, module ID and
sequence number, topology updates, and the commands and configuration decisions
sent by the coordinator.

\gsubsub{Reconfiguration planning}

Reconfiguration planning takes a target configuration from the perception
subsystem together with a set of modules at arbitrary poses, and returns which
module goes where, in what order, and how each one drives there and connects.
It runs in three stages. \emph{Task assignment} maps physical modules to target
positions so as to minimise total travel distance, using the Kuhn--Munkres
algorithm over a cost matrix of relative-pose distances. \emph{Parallel
assembly sequencing} walks the target tree breadth-first from its root and
groups all actions at a given depth into a single group executed concurrently,
reducing the schedule from $O(n)$ sequential docking operations to $O(depth)$
groups --- the central time saving, since docking time, not computation, is the
binding cost: reconfiguration in the reference system costs roughly one minute
per module movement \cite{daudelin2018}, against a planning cost measured in
milliseconds. \emph{Docking} carries each module to a waypoint one segment
short of its parent, aligns the two connectors, and drives straight in until
the electropermanent connectors engage.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Navigation layer.} For the navigation stage Liu et al.\ name,
but do not integrate, the prioritised route planner of Binder et al.\
\cite{binder2019}; we adopt it, and Section~\ref{sec:theory} works out what adopting it entails.
Its routing table attaches a precondition of the form ``robot $x$ has passed
step $n$'' to each segment, so a module waits on a peer's published progress
rather than on wall-clock timing. This is what makes the parallel action groups
of the previous stage executable rather than merely schedulable. The planner is
incomplete, in that prioritised planning can fail on solvable instances; an
action group that cannot be routed after rescheduling therefore degrades to
sequential execution, which costs time but leaves the assembly sequence valid.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Lateral docking retained.} A nonholonomic base cannot
translate sideways into a LEFT/RIGHT mating pose. Liu et al.\ resolve this with
a helper module that docks to the child's TOP face, lifts it clear of the
ground, manoeuvres it into position and sets it down; we implement the same
manoeuvre rather than excluding the connection type. The cost is one module of
the 4--6 budget committed as helper for the duration of any lateral action, and
an actuation requirement --- a module able to raise a peer's weight without
tipping --- that feeds back into the mechanical design of
Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\medskip
\noindent We accept that cost because excluding lateral docking is far more
expensive than it first appears. Each module carries exactly one TOP and one
BOTTOM connector, so restricting edges to TOP--BOTTOM pairs caps every module's
degree at two, and the reachable topologies collapse to linear chains --- no
branching, no side-by-side widening. Measured against the configurations
demonstrated in this lineage, only the chain-type designs would survive: Car,
Scorpion, DoubleDriver and SwerveLifter all require LEFT/RIGHT edges
\cite{daudelin2018,jing2018}. The parallelism argument degrades with them,
since a chain rooted at its centre offers only two actions per depth level.
Retaining lateral docking is what keeps branching trees reachable, and with
them both the published configuration library and the $O(depth)$ schedule that
motivates adopting this framework at all.

\medskip
\noindent One configuration remains out of reach for an unrelated reason:
RollingLoop is a closed cycle, and the framework assembles trees only
(Section~\ref{sec:theory}). The sequencer must reject any target configuration
containing a cycle and return it to configuration selection rather than emit an
infeasible plan.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Module localisation.} Task assignment, route planning and path
following all require module poses expressed in a common frame. For Phase~A we
adopt the scheme demonstrated by Daudelin et al.\ \cite{daudelin2018}: an RGB-D
camera builds the environment map via RTAB-MAP SLAM, and AprilTag fiducials
mounted on each module are read by a downward-facing camera covering the
reconfiguration area to supply module pose during navigation and docking. This
is external perception, consistent with the Phase~A scope; the transition to
onboard odometry and localisation belongs to Phase~D.

\gsubsub{Simulation and assembled locomotion}

Simulation tooling is selected per phase, not once for the project. No single
simulator serves planner validation, driving, contact-rich docking and RL
training equally well, and committing early to one forces compromises in the
others.

\begin{longtable}{@{}L{0.26\textwidth} L{0.22\textwidth} L{0.42\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Need} & \textbf{Tool} & \textbf{Why} \\
\midrule
\endhead
Planner validation (assignment, depth scheduling) &
  matplotlib / plain Python &
  2D kinematics only, no physics required; validated against the published
  task-assignment mapping in Liu et al.\ \cite{liu2023}. \\

  Gazebo Harmonic + ROS\,2 &

  \cite{daudelin2018} --- can be exercised there. MuJoCo does not model these
  sensors, so perception-in-the-loop tests stay on the Gazebo side. \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\noindent\textbf{Integration.} ROS\,2 holds everything in the control loop ---
planner, docking controller, mode supervisor and inter-module messaging.
Training runs outside it, against MJX directly, and trained policies are
wrapped as ROS\,2 nodes afterwards; a thin bridge connects MuJoCo state to
ROS\,2 topics where a policy must run in the loop. Newton / MuJoCo Warp is the
throughput upgrade path should MJX become the bottleneck, and models carry
over, so it is not where we begin.

\noindent\textbf{Each assembled configuration is modelled and trained as a
single robot.} Once docked, a configuration is treated as one fixed-morphology
machine with its own model, its own policy and its own gait, rather than as $n$
modules under a shared policy that generalises across topologies. This makes
assembled locomotion an ordinary single-robot reinforcement-learning problem,
solvable with off-the-shelf PPO and standard tooling, and it is affordable at
our scale. It also mirrors how the established design libraries are organised:
Jing et al.\ \cite{jing2016,jing2018} pair each configuration with its own
hand-authored behaviour, so a per-configuration policy is the learned analogue
of an existing structure rather than an arbitrary simplification. The cost is
that nothing transfers between configurations: a new morphology means a new
model and a new training run from scratch. We accept this because the
configuration set is small and fixed in advance; a topology-conditioned policy
of the kind Whitman et al.\ \cite{whitman2023} describe is the right choice at a
larger configuration count, and is recorded as the scaling path rather than
adopted now.

\noindent\textbf{Two model forms, generated from one source.} Simulating
reconfiguration requires a scene of separate module bodies with weld
constraints toggled at runtime; training a locomotion policy is faster and more
stable on a fixed kinematic tree representing the assembled configuration. We
keep both, and generate the per-configuration model programmatically from the
connection graph defined in Section~\ref{sec:theory} --- the same
representation the planner uses --- so the two cannot drift apart as the module
design changes. The interfaces in the generated model are compliant joints
carrying the measured connector stiffness, not rigid fixed joints: training on
a rigid tree would train the policy on a stiffer robot than the hardware is,
which is the same error the randomisation strategy below exists to avoid.

\noindent\textbf{Control of assembled locomotion} is split on whether a usable
model exists. A configuration whose wheels remain grounded and whose wheel axes
stay parallel after assembly is a rigid body with wheels, and is controlled
classically as a differential drive; no learning is warranted. Configurations
that traverse by contact --- stair climbing, gap bridging --- are trained with
PPO \cite{schulman2017}. The classical case is narrower than ``any
configuration with wheels'': a vertical stack lifts the upper modules' wheels
clear of the ground, so each configuration is classified explicitly rather than
by default.

Every configuration additionally receives a scripted gait, which serves twice:
as the fallback if a learned policy fails to transfer, and as the quantitative
baseline the policy is measured against. This baseline is not a strawman ---
hand-authored open-loop gaits are the state of practice in this lineage, from
PolyBot's gait tables \cite{yim2003} to the manually designed behaviours of the
SMORES-EP design library \cite{jing2016,jing2018} --- so beating it is a result
about the method rather than about our tuning effort. A finding of the form
``traversal success rises from 60\,\% to 85\,\% against the hand-tuned gait''
is defensible; ``our policy works'' is not. With lateral docking implemented,
the reachable set is every tree topology within the module budget, so the
configuration set can be drawn from the published library rather than
restricted to chains: of an expected five configurations we anticipate two
requiring learned policies, the contact-rich ones.

\noindent The scripted fallback also answers where these systems actually fail.
Daudelin et al.\ \cite{daudelin2018} report that of 24 recorded failures of one
demonstration, 41.7\,\% were hardware and 25\,\% perception --- not planning. A
controller that degrades to a known-good open-loop gait when a policy or a
sensor misbehaves is therefore worth more than a marginally better policy.

\noindent\textbf{Transfer} is governed by the post-docking state distribution.
A policy for an assembled configuration inherits whatever pose error
reconfiguration leaves behind, so training randomises precisely that:
per-interface position and orientation offset, joint angles at handover,
approach angle to the obstacle, actuator backlash, and connector compliance
\cite{tobin2017,peng2018}. This is where the locomotion subsystem couples to
the reconfiguration subsystem --- the randomisation ranges are not free
parameters but must be matched to the residual alignment error measured in the
docking trials of Section~\ref{sec:theory}. Note also that the docking mechanism is modelled as a
weld equality constraint toggled at runtime, which supplies the topology
change; the compliance is then obtained by softening that constraint's solver
parameters, not by treating the connection as rigid.

\noindent\textbf{Scope.} One configuration and one obstacle, carried through to
hardware, before a second is added. Gap crossing is the first target: it
transfers more readily than stair climbing, which demonstrates better but
carries the higher transfer risk and is therefore scheduled as the stretch
objective.

\noindent\textbf{First milestone.} Two module bodies in MuJoCo --- weld at one
face, unweld, re-weld at a different face. This is the smallest test of the
entire simulation architecture: if runtime topology change is clean, the
approach holds; if it is not, that is discovered before the detailed module
model is built rather than after.

\gsubsub{Positioning and alignment system}

\noindent\textbf{Concept and theoretical background.} Building on the
self-assembly architecture of SMMRob \cite{li2022} and the IR-based docking
principles of Sambot \cite{wei2011}, this project proposes a cascaded
positioning and alignment system for modular self-reconfigurable robots. The
system divides autonomous docking into different operating ranges, using UWB
and the IMU for macro-scale relative positioning and IR sensing for local
alignment and final docking. Unlike camera-based approaches that require visual
sensing on individual modules, the proposed architecture uses a designated
leader module for positioning, while follower modules do not require onboard
cameras.

\noindent\textbf{Macro-scale relative positioning: UWB + IMU.} During the
initial rendezvous, UWB ranging is combined with high-frequency IMU
measurements to estimate the relative position between leader and follower. An
error-state invariant extended Kalman filter (IEKF) fuses these measurements
and improves robustness against noise and UWB outliers, including
non-line-of-sight errors \cite{zhang2022}. This provides anchor-free relative
positioning without requiring external localisation infrastructure.

\noindent\textbf{Micro-alignment and docking: IR-based alignment.} When the
follower enters the local docking region, the system transitions from UWB-based
positioning to IR-based local alignment, inspired by the IR-based autonomous
docking mechanism demonstrated in Sambot \cite{wei2011}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{figures/image3.png}
  \caption{Two-stage infrared docking scheme: approaching IR for local
           direction, docking IR for close-range alignment, with IMU feedback
           supplying attitude \cite{wei2011}.}
  \label{fig:ir-docking}
\end{figure}

\noindent The proposed IR system uses two functions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Approaching IR:} provides local directional information to
        guide the follower toward the docking interface.
  \item \textbf{Docking IR:} provides close-range alignment feedback to reduce
        lateral and angular misalignment before mechanical connection.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The IMU is additionally used to provide attitude information during
the final alignment process.

%------------------------------------------------------------------- Phase B --
\gsubsection{Phase B --- Docking and task-oriented reconfiguration}

\gsubsub{Docking interface}

This docking interface is developed as the next stage after Phase A, which
focuses on basic locomotion using the SMORES-based module design. The docking
system builds directly on the Phase A module rather than introducing a
completely new mechanical platform.

The EP-Face connector used in SMORES-EP is based on an electropermanent magnet
(EPM), a type of magnet introduced for modular robotics by Knaian
\cite{knaian2010} and later adapted for the SMORES-EP platform by Tosun et al.\
\cite{tosun2016}. Unlike a conventional electromagnet, an EPM can switch
between an attractive and a non-attractive state using a short electrical
pulse, without requiring continuous power to maintain its state.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/image7.png}
  \caption{Electropermanent magnet operating principle and the EP-Face
           connector layout \cite{knaian2010,tosun2016}.}
  \label{fig:epface}
\end{figure}

This makes the EP-Face particularly suitable for our modular self-reconfigurable
robot. In its docked state, the connector can provide a strong holding force
while consuming almost no standby power. SMORES-EP's EP-Face has been reported
to provide up to approximately 90\,N of holding force, while switching requires
only short current pulses \cite{liu2023,tosun2016}. This is useful for our
design because modules may need to remain connected for long periods, while
communication between modules already consumes significant power.

Unlike the locomotion-only Phase A design, where the module does not need to
interact physically with other modules, the new design requires docking from
multiple directions. EP-Faces will therefore be placed on all four sides of the
module. Having identical docking interfaces on all four sides supports
different robot configurations and gives the reconfiguration system greater
freedom when forming chains, larger structures, or other shapes.

Another important advantage is the EP-Face's tolerance to alignment errors. The
EP-Face uses multiple magnets arranged around the connector, allowing two faces
to connect across a range of relative positions and rotations. Reported
tolerances are approximately 4\,mm of separation and 7\,mm of lateral
misalignment \cite{liu2023}. This reduces the precision required from the
robot's movement and alignment system during docking, simplifying the control
system. This is particularly useful when transitioning from Phase A's basic
locomotion to physical interaction, as the locomotion system does not need to
provide extremely precise positioning before a connection can be made.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{figures/image5.png}
  \caption{EP-Face connector geometry and its area of acceptance: the
           genderless magnet ring permits connection across a range of relative
           positions and rotations \cite{tosun2016,liu2023}.}
  \label{fig:epface-tolerance}
\end{figure}

The connector is also genderless, meaning any EP-Face can connect to another
EP-Face without requiring separate male and female interfaces. This is
especially important because any side may act as either the connecting or the
receiving side, depending on the desired configuration. The distributed
magnetic arrangement also provides better resistance to forces and moments than
a single central magnet, which is relevant to our concern about connections
supporting cantilevered chains of modules.

Tosun et al.\ \cite{tosun2016} also demonstrated that the EP-Face can support
communication between connected modules. Although we do not rely on this
feature in the baseline design, it could later be used as a secondary method
for confirming which modules are physically connected to each other.

The EP-Face is selected as our baseline docking mechanism because it combines
low power consumption, strong holding force, alignment tolerance, genderless
connection, and potential face-to-face communication. Placing the interfaces on
all four sides extends the Phase A SMORES-based locomotion module into a
physically interactive modular robot while preserving its basic mechanical
concept.

\gsubsub{Task-oriented planning}

Phase B builds on the existing SMORES-EP reconfiguration pipeline. Once a
target configuration is selected, the existing root selection, task assignment,
and parallel docking process can proceed as described by Liu et al.\
\cite{liu2023}. The main addition in Phase B is therefore a new layer that
determines which configuration should be used based on the task, rather than
relying on a fixed shape.

The input to this layer can come in two forms. The first is a structured task
requirement, such as the required reach, gap width, or payload capacity. The
second is raw environmental information, such as a depth image or height map,
from which these task requirements would first need to be estimated. Since
selecting an incorrect configuration can be difficult and costly to test on
real hardware, the system should be developed in stages rather than starting
with a complex learning model.

The first stage may use a rule-based approach with a small library of
predefined configurations. Each configuration will have a known capability
range, such as maximum reach, gap-crossing ability, or load capacity. The
planner will compare the task requirements with these capabilities and select
the smallest configuration that can complete the task. This follows the
discrete-library approach used by Daudelin et al.\ \cite{daudelin2018} while
avoiding the need for training data. It is also easier to understand and verify
before being used on the physical robot.

The second stage can replace the fixed library with a graph neural network
(GNN) if the range of supported tasks becomes too large for a rule-based
system. Since the robot configuration is already represented as a graph, a GNN
can work directly with this representation instead of first converting the
configuration into an image or other spatial representation. The same
structural constraints used by the existing reconfiguration pipeline, such as
tree topology and orientation constraints, can therefore be maintained
\cite{liu2023}.

A 3D CNN will be considered as a later stage if raw environmental sensor data
becomes the main input. In this case, the CNN would be used primarily for
perception, converting the sensed environment into task requirements such as
required reach or gap width. It would not directly generate the final
configuration. This keeps the configuration-selection process separate from
environmental perception and allows the same selection system to be used
regardless of where the task requirements originate.

The rule-based configuration library is selected as the baseline because it
requires no training data, is easy to verify, and provides a simple starting
point for testing on real hardware. The GNN approach is planned as a later
extension because it works directly with the graph structure already used by
the reconfiguration system. A 3D CNN is reserved for a future stage where raw
environmental perception becomes necessary, allowing it to act as a perception
front end rather than replacing the existing configuration-selection process.

%------------------------------------------------------------------- Phase C --
\gsubsection{Phase C --- Onboard perception with external computation}

\gsubsub{Concept and theoretical background}

For modular self-reconfigurable robots such as SMORES-EP, autonomous
exploration in unstructured environments requires onboard perception to support
navigation and morphological adaptation. Conventional RGB-D cameras can provide
rich depth information but may introduce additional size, weight, power and
computational requirements. This project therefore proposes a low-SWaP
perception system combining a compact monocular RGB camera with an $8\times8$
multi-zone time-of-flight (ToF) sensor. During the prototype stage, the sensors
are mounted on the leader module, while sensor data are transmitted over a
wired link to an external processing unit for real-time processing.

\gsubsub{Terrain geometry perception using multi-zone ToF}

An $8\times8$ multi-zone ToF sensor, such as the VL53L5CX, is used to provide a
compact depth representation of the local environment. M\"uller et al.\
\cite{muller2023} demonstrated that a 64-pixel multi-zone ToF sensor can
provide lightweight depth-based perception for autonomous navigation on a
resource-constrained robotic platform, using a decision-tree-based approach
with low computational and power requirements.

For the proposed MSRR, the ToF measurements are processed to estimate local
geometric characteristics such as obstacle distance, height discontinuities,
slope, and surface variation. These features provide a compact representation
of the surrounding terrain without requiring dense point-cloud processing.

\gsubsub{Monocular vision for supplementary terrain perception}

A compact monocular RGB camera is used as a supplementary sensor to provide
visual information about the environment. The camera is not intended for
complex object bounding-box detection (e.g.\ YOLO) at this stage. Instead, a
lightweight vision model will extract broad visual textures and semantic
classifications of the surface --- differentiating, for example, between a
smooth floor, rough gravel, and vegetation. This visual texture information is
then combined with ToF measurements to improve the overall perception of the
local terrain and of traversable regions. This approach is motivated by
previous terrain-perception research demonstrating that fusing visual semantics
with geometric information significantly enhances the estimation of terrain
properties and the construction of reliable traversability maps for robot
navigation \cite{haddeler2022}.

%------------------------------------------------------------------- Phase D --
\gsubsection{Phase D --- Fully onboard perception and computation}

\gsubsub{Compute module}

The compute module is added in the last phase, once locomotion, docking and
communication are working. It is a separate board from the joint-control MCU
and carries the camera and the perception software, so that the MCU keeps its
real-time work while the compute module handles situation classification and
configuration selection. We plan to fit it only to the module acting as
coordinator, rather than to every module, following CKBot, which uses one
dedicated camera module per cluster \cite{yim2007ckbot}; a camera on every
module would cost power, volume and money we do not need at this scale.
Candidates are the Raspberry Pi CM5 ($55\times40\times5$\,mm) and the STM32N6,
which has an integrated NPU for lower power. The interface to the module MCU
carries decisions and setpoints only.

\gsection{Theory and Technical Backup}\label{sec:theory}

\pending{%
The theory below is what the starting period requires: the relations that size
the hardware, bound what the electronics must achieve, and decide whether a
design choice is feasible before anything is built. Each subsection states the
governing relation and what it is used to fix.
}

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\pending{\gsubsection{Locomotion mechanism design}}

\pending{%
\noindent\textbf{Driving on the ground.} A module on its two side wheels is a
differential drive. Body speed and yaw rate follow from the wheel angular
velocities $\omega_L$, $\omega_R$ and the wheel radius $r$ over track width
$b$:
\begin{equation}
  v = \frac{r}{2}\left(\omega_R + \omega_L\right),
  \qquad
  \dot{\psi} = \frac{r}{b}\left(\omega_R - \omega_L\right).
\end{equation}
Setting $\omega_R = -\omega_L$ gives $v = 0$ with $\dot{\psi} \neq 0$: the
zero-radius turn used to correct heading immediately before docking, when the
module must rotate without translating out of the connector's capture envelope.

\noindent\textbf{Joint torque sets the module count.} Once docked, the same
degrees of freedom act as joints, and the binding case is the cantilever. For a
chain of $n$ modules of mass $m$ at pitch $L$ held horizontally, the root joint
carries the summed moment of every module outboard of it:
\begin{equation}
  \tau_{\text{root}} = mgL\,\frac{n(n+1)}{2}.
  \label{eq:cantilever}
\end{equation}
The quadratic growth in $n$ is the reason module count cannot be chosen
independently of actuator selection. This is compared against the delivered
joint torque
\begin{equation}
  \tau_{\text{out}} = \tau_{\text{motor}}\,N\,\eta ,
\end{equation}
for gear ratio $N$ and drivetrain efficiency $\eta$, and the largest $n$
satisfying $\tau_{\text{out}} \geq \tau_{\text{root}}$ is the characteristic
strength of the design. Because the tilt degree of freedom combines both
motors' torque through the crown gear (Section~\ref{sec:concept}), tilt is the
axis this analysis is applied to.

Pan and tilt are not independently actuated. Both come from the same two
motors through the 48-tooth crown gear, which makes the mechanism a differential
gear train: counter-rotating the two inner spur gears pans the crown gear about
DoF~3, co-rotating them tilts it about DoF~4 \cite{davey2012}. Writing
$\theta_1,\theta_2$ for the two driven spur-gear angles, the joint angles are
their difference and their sum,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{bmatrix} \theta_{\text{pan}} \\ \theta_{\text{tilt}} \end{bmatrix}
  = \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} k_p & -k_p \\ k_t & k_t \end{bmatrix}}_{\textstyle \mathbf{T}}
    \begin{bmatrix} \theta_1 \\ \theta_2 \end{bmatrix},
  \label{eq:diff}
\end{equation}
with $k_p$, $k_t$ the mesh ratios of the two paths through the crown gear. The
fundamental train equation for a differential gives these ratios from the tooth
counts \cite{tsai1999}.

The torque relation is the transpose of \eqref{eq:diff}, which follows from
virtual work and holds whatever the gear ratios turn out to be:
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\theta} = \mathbf{T}^{\!\top}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{q}
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \boldsymbol{\tau}_{q} = \mathbf{T}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\theta}.
\end{equation}
Tilt therefore draws on the sum of the two motor torques and pan on their
difference, which is why tilt is the stronger axis and why
\eqref{eq:cantilever} is applied to it. SMORES measured 2.3\,N$\cdot$m in tilt
against 1.4\,N$\cdot$m in pan at 9\,V \cite{davey2012}; the ratio is 1.64
rather than 2 because the two paths through the crown gear have different mesh
ratios and different losses. Reproducing those two numbers on our own module is
the check that \eqref{eq:diff} has been implemented as intended.

Coupling also changes the dynamics, because motor inertia no longer reflects
into one joint at a time. With $\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{T}\boldsymbol{\theta}$ and
motor inertia matrix $\mathbf{I}_m$, the rigid-body model in joint coordinates
becomes
\begin{equation}
  \left(\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q}) + \mathbf{T}^{-\top}\mathbf{I}_m\mathbf{T}^{-1}\right)\ddot{\mathbf{q}}
  + \mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})\dot{\mathbf{q}}
  + \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{q})
  = \mathbf{T}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\theta},
  \label{eq:coupleddyn}
\end{equation}
the standard transmission-coupled form \cite{siciliano2009,craig2005}. The
off-diagonal terms of $\mathbf{T}^{-\top}\mathbf{I}_m\mathbf{T}^{-1}$ are the
practical consequence: a pan command disturbs tilt and vice versa, so the two
axes cannot be tuned as independent single-input loops. Either the controller
is written in motor coordinates $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, where the axes decouple,
or it compensates the coupling explicitly. We will take the first route and
treat \eqref{eq:diff} as the interface between planner and joint controller.

\noindent\textbf{Motor selection} works from the DC torque--speed
characteristic, linear between stall torque $\tau_s$ at zero speed and no-load
speed $\omega_0$ at zero torque, with peak mechanical power at the midpoint.
The operating point must sit clear of stall for the cantilever case while still
delivering the driving speed the traverse requires --- the two demands that run
through the same gear train, and the reason the gearing is a compromise rather
than an optimum for either.

\noindent\textbf{Traction, not torque, bounds gradeability.} On an incline
$\alpha$ the tractive force required is
\begin{equation}
  F_{\text{req}} = mg\left(\mu_{\text{roll}}\cos\alpha + \sin\alpha\right),
\end{equation}
while the ground can supply no more than $\mu_s mg\cos\alpha$. Above a critical
grade the wheels slip regardless of available torque, so the obstacle
parameters of Section~\ref{sec:objectives} must be checked against friction as
well as against \eqref{eq:cantilever}.

\noindent\textbf{Assembled gaits.} A chain traverses by propagating a
travelling wave along its joints,
\begin{equation}
  \theta_i(t) = A\sin\!\left(2\pi f t + \phi_i\right),
  \qquad \phi_i = \frac{2\pi i}{n},
\end{equation}
with amplitude $A$ and frequency $f$ setting stride and speed. Configurations
that lift modules clear of the ground are statically stable only while the
projection of the centre of mass stays inside the support polygon, which is the
constraint that limits how far an arch can reach before it topples.
}

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\pending{\gsubsection{Communication system (data transmission)}}

\pending{%
\noindent\textbf{Load against capacity.} Offered load is payload size times
update rate times module count. Compared against nominal radio capacity this
looks comfortable, but it is the wrong comparison: the traffic is many small
frames, not few large ones, so the binding limit is channel airtime under
CSMA/CA, where per-frame overhead and contention grow with the number of nodes
rather than with payload size. The quantity to design against is therefore
frames per second across the cluster, not bits per second.

\noindent\textbf{Latency is fixed by the connector, not by preference.} A
module closing on a docking face at approach speed $v_{\text{approach}}$
accumulates position error for as long as it takes a measurement to become an
actuator response. For total loop latency $t_{\text{total}}$ the residual error
at contact is
\begin{equation}
  e = v_{\text{approach}}\,t_{\text{total}},
\end{equation}
so for a connector with area of acceptance $a$ the requirement is
\begin{equation}
  t_{\text{total}} \le \frac{a}{v_{\text{approach}}}.
\end{equation}
The EP-Face tolerances quoted in Section~\ref{sec:concept} therefore convert
directly into a latency budget --- and, read the other way, a slower approach
buys latency at the cost of docking time.

\noindent\textbf{Synchronisation for coordinated gaits.} Joints on separate
modules running a common travelling wave must share a time base. A clock offset
$\Delta t$ between two modules appears as a phase error
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\phi = 2\pi f\,\Delta t ,
\end{equation}
so the tolerable offset scales inversely with gait frequency: faster gaits
demand tighter synchronisation.

\noindent\textbf{Topology is inferred, not measured.} The cluster's
connectivity graph is assembled from per-face neighbour detection, and each
module's structural position is then obtained by propagating coordinates
outward from a root over that graph. No module measures its own position in the
assembly; it derives it from who its neighbours are and on which faces.
}

\pending{\gsubsection{Low-level control and sensing}}

\pending{%
\noindent\textbf{Motor model.} Both joint control and current-based torque
feedback rest on the DC motor equations
\begin{equation}
  V = IR + L\frac{dI}{dt} + K_e\omega,
  \qquad
  \tau = K_t I ,
\end{equation}
which state that torque is proportional to current --- the basis for sensing
load without a torque sensor.

\noindent\textbf{Cascaded control.} Each joint runs position over velocity over
current. The loops are separated in bandwidth so that the inner loop settles
well within the outer loop's sample period; without that separation the loops
interact and the joint oscillates. PWM through an H-bridge sets average motor
voltage by duty cycle, giving bidirectional control from a unipolar supply.

\noindent\textbf{How the encoder recovers angle.} An incremental encoder emits
two square waves, A and B, in quadrature --- B lagging A by a quarter cycle.
Over one cycle the pair passes through four distinct states, $(A,B) =
00 \to 01 \to 11 \to 10$, and a decoder that counts every edge rather than
every cycle therefore resolves four counts per line. For $N_\ell$ lines on the
disc and gear ratio $N$ between encoder and joint, joint resolution is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\theta = \frac{2\pi}{4 N_\ell N} \ \text{rad/count},
\end{equation}
and quantisation alone bounds position error at $\pm\Delta\theta/2$. Direction
comes from the order of the transition, not from the count: the sequence above
run backwards is $00 \to 10 \to 11 \to 01$, so a two-bit state machine
comparing the present state with the previous one yields sign as well as
magnitude \cite{alciatore2011}. Velocity is then obtained either by counting
edges in a fixed window, which is accurate at speed and coarse near zero, or by
timing the interval between edges, which behaves the other way round; the joint
controller uses the second because docking happens slowly.

A magnetic encoder is absolute rather than incremental. The sensor reports two
signals in spatial quadrature, and the shaft angle follows directly:
\begin{equation}
  \phi = \operatorname{atan2}\!\left(V_{\sin},\, V_{\cos}\right).
\end{equation}
There is no index pulse to hunt for at power-up and no count to lose if a step
is missed --- both properties matter for a module that may be re-energised in
an arbitrary pose after reconfiguration. This is the reason for moving away
from the original SMORES continuous-rotation potentiometers, which resolved
5$^\circ$ \cite{davey2012}.

\noindent\textbf{Current sensing must be synchronous.} Current is measured as a
voltage across a shunt, $V = IR_{\text{shunt}}$, amplified before conversion.
The shunt carries motor current only during part of the switching cycle, so an
asynchronous sample returns a value that depends on where in the PWM period it
happened to land. The ADC conversion must therefore be triggered at a fixed
point in the PWM period --- a timing requirement on the microcontroller, not
merely a wiring choice, and one of the reasons for preferring a part with
timer-triggered ADC.

\noindent\textbf{Attitude from the IMU.} With the module static, the
accelerometer reads gravity alone and the two tilt angles follow from its three
axes:
\begin{equation}
  \phi_{\text{acc}} = \operatorname{atan2}\!\left(a_y,\, a_z\right),
  \qquad
  \theta_{\text{acc}} = \operatorname{atan2}\!\left(-a_x,\, \sqrt{a_y^2 + a_z^2}\right).
\end{equation}
Roll and pitch are observable this way; yaw is not, because rotation about the
gravity vector does not change the measurement. That is why heading has to come
from the wheel odometry or from the positioning subsystem rather than from the
IMU alone.

The estimate above is useless during motion, since the accelerometer cannot
separate gravity from the module's own acceleration. Integrating the gyroscope
instead,
\begin{equation}
  \phi_{\text{gyro}}(k) = \phi(k-1) + \left(\omega_x(k) - b_x\right)\Delta t ,
\end{equation}
is clean over short intervals but accumulates: any residual bias error
$\tilde{b}_x$ grows without bound as $\tilde{b}_x t$, and the random walk on
top of it grows as $\sqrt{t}$. Neither sensor is sufficient alone, and their
errors sit in opposite parts of the spectrum --- the accelerometer is noisy but
unbiased, the gyroscope smooth but drifting.

A complementary filter exploits exactly that split, low-passing the
accelerometer and high-passing the integrated rate \cite{higgins1975}:
\begin{equation}
  \phi(k) = \alpha\left[\phi(k-1) + \omega_x(k)\,\Delta t\right]
          + (1-\alpha)\,\phi_{\text{acc}}(k),
  \qquad
  \alpha = \frac{\tau}{\tau + \Delta t},
  \label{eq:compfilter}
\end{equation}
where the single tuning constant $\tau$ is the crossover time: below it the
gyroscope is trusted, above it the accelerometer. A Kalman filter does the same
job with the weighting derived from the noise covariances instead of chosen by
hand, and additionally estimates the gyroscope bias $b_x$ as a state rather
than assuming it away. We will start with \eqref{eq:compfilter} because it has
one parameter and can be verified on a bench tilt table, and move to the
nonlinear complementary filter on $SO(3)$ \cite{mahony2008} or a gradient-
descent formulation \cite{madgwick2011} if the planar form proves insufficient
once the module is climbing rather than driving.
}

\pending{\gsubsection{Positioning and alignment system}}

\pending{%
\noindent\textbf{Relative positioning and autonomous docking.} An MSRR must
determine the relative position and orientation of its modules before they can
connect. Docking therefore divides into two tasks: \emph{relative positioning},
which brings the modules into each other's vicinity, and \emph{local
alignment}, which reduces the remaining positional and angular error far enough
for mechanical connection.

The proposed system uses different sensing for the two stages --- UWB and IMU
for longer-range relative positioning, infrared for short-range alignment. Each
sensor is used across the range where its characteristics are best, which
removes the need for any one technology to cover the whole docking process.

\noindent\textbf{UWB-based relative positioning.} Ultra-wideband sensing gives
the relative distance between two modules from the propagation time of radio
signals between their transceivers. In its simplest form the measured distance
is
\begin{equation}
  d_{\text{UWB}} = \left\lVert \mathbf{p}_L - \mathbf{p}_F \right\rVert + v,
  \label{eq:uwb}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{p}_L$ and $\mathbf{p}_F$ are the positions of the leader and
follower modules and $v$ is measurement error.

UWB suits the initial positioning stage because it supplies relative range
without visual line-of-sight detection, which matters for a modular system
operating where fixed external positioning infrastructure is unavailable.
Its weakness is noise and signal obstruction: non-line-of-sight interference
produces inaccurate or outlier measurements. That is what motivates fusion
rather than relying on UWB alone \cite{zhang2022}.

\noindent\textbf{IMU-based motion and orientation estimation.} An inertial
measurement unit supplies linear acceleration and angular velocity at high
rate, from which the module's motion and orientation are estimated between UWB
updates. UWB gives relative distance but not a continuous estimate of the full
relative motion; the IMU propagates motion information across the gaps. Since
inertial estimation drifts as error accumulates, combining the two exploits
strengths that sit in opposite parts of the spectrum.

\noindent\textbf{Fusion by invariant error-state Kalman filter.} The two
measurement streams are fused in an error-state invariant extended Kalman
filter (IEKF), with a prediction step driven by the IMU and a measurement
update from UWB:
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{x}_{k\mid k-1} &= f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{k-1},\, \mathbf{u}_k\right),
  \label{eq:iekf-predict}\\
  \mathbf{z}_k &= h\!\left(\mathbf{x}_k\right) + \mathbf{v}_k,
  \label{eq:iekf-update}
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{x}$ is the estimated state, $\mathbf{u}_k$ the inertial
measurements, $\mathbf{z}_k$ the UWB observation and $\mathbf{v}_k$ the
measurement noise. The invariant error-state formulation gives a
mathematically suitable representation for relative pose estimation and
improves robustness under measurement disturbance. State definition,
propagation model, covariance equations and the full update procedure are
developed in Section~\ref{sec:concept}; the use of UWB--IMU fusion for
anchor-free relative positioning follows established relative-positioning work
\cite{zhang2022}.

\noindent\textbf{IR-based local alignment.} As the follower approaches, the
accuracy demanded rises sharply, because mechanical docking requires the
interfaces to be aligned within the connector's capture envelope. Short-range
infrared sensing covers this stage, supplying proximity and directional
information from emitted or reflected signals. It is well suited to confirming
that the follower is approaching the docking interface correctly and to
providing local feedback during final alignment; earlier modular systems have
demonstrated IR-guided autonomous docking \cite{wei2011}. The stage divides
into two functions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Approaching IR} --- local directional information guiding the
        follower toward the docking interface.
  \item \emph{Docking IR} --- close-range feedback that removes the remaining
        lateral and angular misalignment immediately before connection.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The IMU supplies orientation throughout this stage, so the controller
can resolve translational and rotational alignment together.
}

\pending{%
\noindent\textbf{The cascaded architecture.} Taken together the stages form
one sequence rather than three independent systems:
}

\begin{pblock}
\begin{longtable}{@{}L{0.24\textwidth} L{0.22\textwidth} L{0.46\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Stage} & \textbf{Main sensors} & \textbf{Purpose} \\
\midrule
\endhead
Macro-scale positioning & UWB + IMU &
  Estimate relative state and guide the follower toward the target. \\
Local approach & Approaching IR &
  Obtain local directional information. \\
Final alignment & Docking IR + IMU &
  Reduce positional and angular error before mechanical docking. \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}
\end{pblock}

\pending{%
\noindent The UWB--IMU and IR systems overlap rather than hand over cleanly:
UWB and IMU produce the relative-state estimate needed to manoeuvre into the
docking region, after which IR supplies the high-resolution local feedback
that final mechanical alignment requires. The transition threshold between
stages, the alignment-error formulation, the sensor arrangement, the control
strategy and the mechanical docking conditions are all to be fixed
experimentally.
}

\pending{\gsubsection{Docking interface}}

\pending{%
\noindent\textbf{Magnetic circuit and holding-force model.} An electropermanent
magnet pairs a hard (AlNiCo-type) and a semi-hard (NdFeB-type) magnet sharing a
common flux path. A short current pulse through a coil around the semi-hard
element reverses its polarity. With the two elements aligned their fields add
and flux crosses the air gap to a mating face; opposed, the fields cancel
inside the circuit and negligible flux reaches the gap. Because the magnetic
state is set by remanence rather than by sustained current, holding force does
not scale with continuous power draw. That is the property Tosun et al.\
exploit in the EP-Face \cite{tosun2016}, and the reason the standby-power
argument of Section~\ref{sec:concept} holds.

For a single pole pair with air-gap flux density $B_g$ and pole face area $A$,
the attractive force follows from the Maxwell stress tensor evaluated at the
gap,
\begin{equation}
  F \approx \frac{B_g^2 A}{2\mu_0},
  \qquad \mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}\ \text{H/m}.
  \label{eq:maxwell}
\end{equation}
The EP-Face ring places $n$ such pole pairs around the connector rather than
one central magnet (Figure~\ref{fig:epface-tolerance}). Treating the poles as
acting in parallel,
\begin{equation}
  F_{\text{total}} \approx \frac{n\,B_g^2 A_{\text{pole}}}{2\mu_0}.
  \label{eq:ftotal}
\end{equation}
The reported $\approx$\,90\,N figure for SMORES-EP \cite{liu2023,tosun2016} is
therefore consistent with several small, low-flux poles rather than one large
one --- which is also what keeps the switching pulse energy low, since stored
magnetic energy in each gap scales with pole volume rather than directly with
total holding force. Equation~\eqref{eq:ftotal} is the quantity our own
connector sizing must reproduce or exceed once the face dimensions are fixed by
the module envelope.

\noindent\textbf{Cantilever moment capacity.} Tilt is the strongest degree of
freedom because it combines both motors' torque, which is what lets it carry
the cantilever load in a chain. The connector has to survive that load too: it
is what keeps the cantilevered chain attached to its supporting module in the
first place.

Model a horizontal chain of $k$ cantilevered modules extending from a fixed
dock as point loads $mg$ at module centres, at distances $d_1,\dots,d_k$ from
the supporting face. The bending moment the connector must react is
\begin{equation}
  M = \sum_{i=1}^{k} m\,g\,d_i .
  \label{eq:dockmoment}
\end{equation}
A distributed ring of magnets resists an applied moment the way a bolt-circle
flange does, pulling one side of the ring into greater compression and the
opposite side toward separation, with the reaction distribution approximately
linear across the diameter. For a ring of effective radius $r$ the moment
capacity before any pole separates is
\begin{equation}
  M_{\max} \approx F_{\text{total}}\, r ,
  \label{eq:mmax}
\end{equation}
which is the uniform-ring reduction of an integral of local pole force times
moment arm. This is why a distributed genderless connector was preferred over a
single central magnet: a central magnet's moment capacity is set only by
friction at the contact face, not by a ring radius, and is considerably weaker
for the same axial holding force.

What \eqref{eq:dockmoment} and \eqref{eq:mmax} leave open is the design
constant that the torque analysis behind the obstacle table of
Section~\ref{sec:objectives} must close: the maximum chain length $k$, and the
maximum obstacle span the arch or cantilever configuration can carry, before
$M$ exceeds $M_{\max}$ at whichever fails first, the weakest joint or the
connector. That comparison, not an assumed value, is what should set the
held-out parameter boundary.

\noindent\textbf{Alignment tolerance budget.} The EP-Face acceptance window ---
roughly 4\,mm of separation and 7\,mm of lateral misalignment \cite{liu2023}
--- is a margin, not a target. The positioning and alignment system must
deliver docking-attempt error inside that window with high confidence, not
merely on average. Treating the UWB/IMU macro-positioning error, the IR
micro-alignment error and mechanical backlash as approximately independent,
they combine at contact in root-sum-square:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{\text{total}} = \sqrt{\sigma_{\text{UWB/IMU}}^2
    + \sigma_{\text{IR}}^2 + \sigma_{\text{backlash}}^2}.
  \label{eq:rss}
\end{equation}
A conventional $3\sigma$ margin then requires $3\sigma_{\text{total}} \le
7$\,mm laterally and $3\sigma_{\text{total}} \le 4$\,mm in separation at the
moment the connector is commanded to engage.

That gives a concrete acceptance test for the cascaded positioning system
during Phase~B bring-up: log the achieved $\sigma$ at each stage transition
--- UWB hand-off to IR, IR hand-off to contact --- and confirm the budget
closes before relying on the $\geq$\,90\,\% dock/undock criterion of
Section~\ref{sec:objectives}. A docking system that only occasionally falls
inside the acceptance window cannot clear that bar regardless of how strong the
connector itself is.
}

\pending{\gsubsection{Task-oriented planning}}

\pending{%
\noindent\textbf{Formal problem statement.} Configuration selection takes its
input in one of two forms: a structured task-requirement vector, or raw sensor
data from which that vector must first be estimated. Formalising the first case
gives the baseline the rule-based library has to satisfy.

Let the task requirement be a vector $t \in \mathbb{R}^p$; for our obstacle
classes $p = 1$, being gap width, step height or passage width. Let
$\mathcal{L} = \{c_1,\dots,c_m\}$ be the finite library of pre-verified
configurations --- single module, linear chain, arch/cantilever, and the
optional non-planar fourth. Each configuration carries a capability function
$\operatorname{Cap}(c_i) \in \mathbb{R}^p$ giving the largest obstacle instance
it can clear, such as an arch's maximum gap width derived from
\eqref{eq:mmax}. The rule-based selector is then
\begin{equation}
  c^{\ast} = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{c_i \in \mathcal{L}\,:\,
    \operatorname{Cap}(c_i)\, \geq\, t} \operatorname{Cost}(c_i),
  \label{eq:selector}
\end{equation}
subject to the feasibility constraint that $c_i$'s connection graph is a tree,
since the sequencer assembles trees only and cannot close a cycle; with the
helper-module manoeuvre implemented, lateral (L/R) edges are routable and are
no longer excluded, at the cost of reserving a module per lateral action.
Taking
$\operatorname{Cost}$ as module count --- or equivalently assembly depth, since
parallel assembly time scales with tree depth rather than module count ---
reduces selection to a bounded linear scan of $O(|\mathcal{L}|\,p)$, evaluated
once per obstacle encounter. That is deliberately far cheaper than the
graph-matching alternatives surveyed in Section~\ref{sec:litreview}, which is
consistent with treating those as later stages rather than as the baseline.

\noindent\textbf{Why the capability function must be closed-form.} The
80\,\% selection target is measured explicitly \emph{including held-out
obstacle parameters}: the selector must generalise past the training range, not
memorise it. A library whose $\operatorname{Cap}(c_i)$ values are fitted post
hoc from simulation trials at a few sampled parameter values cannot be trusted
to extrapolate. A library whose values are derived from the underlying
mechanics --- \eqref{eq:mmax} for the arch, an analogous static-equilibrium
relation for the step --- extrapolates by construction, because the same
equation applies outside the sampled range.

This is the reason for developing the moment model before the simulation
campaign rather than after. The model \emph{is} the capability function the
selector consults, and its validity outside the training range is precisely
what the held-out success criterion tests.

\noindent\textbf{Graph representation and the GNN extension.} A graph neural
network is proposed as the second stage, once the task space outgrows a fixed
library, operating directly on the \textsc{Face2Con} connection-graph notation
already adopted for the reconfiguration planner \cite{liu2017}. Framing it now
rather than deferring it entirely clarifies what the rule-based stage must log
to make the transition possible.

Represent a configuration as a graph $G = (V,E)$, one node per module and one
edge per active docking connection, each edge labelled with the relative face
and orientation. A message-passing network updates each node embedding by
aggregating over its neighbours,
\begin{equation}
  h_v^{(k+1)} = \phi\!\left(h_v^{(k)},\ \bigoplus_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)}
    \psi\!\left(h_v^{(k)},\, h_u^{(k)},\, e_{uv}\right)\right),
  \label{eq:gnn}
\end{equation}
for learned $\phi,\psi$ and a permutation-invariant aggregator $\bigoplus$ such
as sum or max. Permutation invariance is the operative property. It means the
same network evaluates a configuration correctly regardless of which physical
module ID occupies which graph position, so the network does not need
retraining when the Kuhn--Munkres task assignment changes which module plays
which role.

A configuration's final embedding, pooled over all $h_v^{(K)}$, is trained to
regress $\operatorname{Cap}(c)$ or to classify suitability directly against
$t$, replacing the library lookup once the library grows too coarse or too
large to enumerate. Because this shares the input representation of the
existing reconfiguration pipeline, introducing the second stage needs no new
perception-to-planner interface --- only the selection function changes, which
is the property the phased design is meant to preserve.
}

\pending{\gsubsection{Built-in camera for perception}}

\pending{%
\noindent\textbf{What the perception stack has to deliver.} To decide whether a
region is traversable, the robot needs local terrain information of two
different kinds: where the surfaces are, and what they are made of. The system
therefore carries two complementary sensors --- a multi-zone time-of-flight
(ToF) sensor for geometry and a compact monocular RGB camera for appearance.
In the prototype both are mounted on the leader module only, and raw data is
carried over a wired link to an external computer. Decoupling the sensing
hardware from the computing hardware in this way lets the perception algorithms
be developed and evaluated in Phase~C without a high-performance compute unit
on every module, which is the Phase~D problem.

\noindent\textbf{Geometric perception from a multi-zone ToF sensor.} A ToF
sensor recovers distance from the round-trip time of an emitted light pulse,
\begin{equation}
  d = \frac{c\,\Delta t}{2},
\end{equation}
where $c$ is the speed of light and $\Delta t$ the round-trip time. A
multi-zone device resolves this separately over discrete regions of its field
of view rather than returning one number, so an $8\times8$ part yields a compact
depth matrix
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{D} = \begin{bmatrix}
    d_{1,1} & \cdots & d_{1,8} \\
    \vdots  & \ddots & \vdots  \\
    d_{8,1} & \cdots & d_{8,8}
  \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
This is the useful middle ground: enough structure to measure obstacle
proximity, depth discontinuity and local surface variation --- the quantities
the governing obstacle parameters of Section~\ref{sec:objectives} are read from
--- without the cost of building and carrying a dense point cloud. A part such
as the VL53L5CX provides 64-zone ranging in a package small enough for a module
of this size, and 64-zone ToF sensing has already been shown to support
obstacle avoidance on severely resource-constrained platforms
\cite{muller2023}.

\noindent\textbf{Visual perception from a monocular camera.} The camera is not
there to run general-purpose object detection. Its role is to supply the
appearance information the ToF sensor cannot: colour, texture and surface
type. A lightweight model extracts broad visual features or coarse surface
categories relevant to traversability --- distinguishing smooth flooring from
loose gravel, for instance, while the ToF sensor supplies the corresponding
slope and depth. Which features and which classifier are used is left to be
fixed during development, against the compute budget and the training data
actually available.

\noindent\textbf{Fusing the two.} The two modalities are combined into a single
local terrain representation,
\begin{equation}
  F_{\mathrm{terrain}} = f\!\left(G_{\mathrm{ToF}},\, V_{\mathrm{RGB}}\right),
\end{equation}
with $G_{\mathrm{ToF}}$ the geometric features from the depth matrix and
$V_{\mathrm{RGB}}$ the visual features from the image. Fusing geometry with
semantics is established practice in terrain analysis for legged navigation
\cite{haddeler2022}; what differs here is the sensing budget. That work relies
on a substantial sensing payload, whereas this system has to reach a usable
traversability estimate from a low size, weight and power configuration that a
single module can carry.
}

\begin{pblock}
\begin{longtable}{@{}L{0.18\textwidth} L{0.19\textwidth} L{0.35\textwidth}
                    L{0.17\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Component} & \textbf{Sensor} & \textbf{Main function} &
\textbf{Processing} \\
\midrule
\endhead
Geometric perception & $8\times8$ multi-zone ToF &
  Depth matrix; obstacle proximity, discontinuities, surface variation &
  External computer \\
Visual perception & Monocular RGB camera &
  Surface appearance, texture, semantic visual features &
  External computer \\
Perception fusion & ToF + RGB &
  Fused local terrain and traversability representation &
  External computer \\
Communication & Wired link &
  High-bandwidth transfer of raw sensor data &
  Wired interface \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}
\end{pblock}

\pending{%
\noindent This is the Phase~C starting architecture, and it is deliberately the
conservative one: it keeps the sensing hardware inside the module envelope
while leaving computation off-board, so that the perception work is not blocked
on the compute-module selection. Sensor placement, calibration, data
synchronisation, the feature-extraction pipeline, the fusion function $f$ and
the decision logic above it are developed and evaluated as part of the
methodology.
}

\pending{\gsubsection{Compute module}}

\pending{%
\noindent\textbf{Camera geometry.} Perception rests on the pinhole model, in
which a point $\mathbf{X}$ in the camera frame projects to image coordinates
through the intrinsic matrix
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{K}\,\mathbf{X},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{K} = \begin{bmatrix}
    f_x & 0 & c_x \\ 0 & f_y & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1
  \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
with calibration required to recover $\mathbf{K}$ and the lens distortion
coefficients before any metric measurement is trusted.

\noindent\textbf{Pose from one image.} The relative pose of a docking face
carrying a planar fiducial is a perspective-$n$-point problem: four known
coplanar points and their image projections determine the full six-degree-of-freedom
pose of the target from a single frame, with no stereo baseline and no depth
sensor. This is what allows a single monocular camera to serve alignment.

\noindent\textbf{The real-time constraint is detection rate against control
rate.} A comparable modular-robot system was limited to roughly 4\,Hz by the
detection step \cite{jing2018}, which is slow enough to dominate the docking
latency budget derived above. The theory required therefore includes the
computational cost of the detection algorithm and how it scales with image
resolution and with the size of the search region --- restricting the search to
a predicted neighbourhood is the standard route to raising the rate without
new hardware.

\noindent\textbf{Configuration selection as classification.} Selecting a
configuration is treated as a mapping from the perceived obstacle state to an
entry in the design library, which makes it a classification problem with a
small, fixed label set --- the reason a rule-based baseline is defensible
before any learned model is introduced.

\noindent\textbf{Inference on an embedded target.} Running that model on the
module rather than off-board requires quantisation from floating point to
fixed-point or integer arithmetic to fit the memory and the arithmetic units of
an embedded NPU. The relevant theory is the accuracy cost of quantisation
against the latency and power saved, since this is the trade that decides
whether Phase~D is achievable on the candidate compute modules.
}

all four obstacle morphologies are trees.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Interface to configuration selection.} The planner receives a
target configuration, not an obstacle. Upstream, the established mechanism is a
property-indexed library \cite{jing2016,jing2018}: each entry pairs a
configuration $C$ and a behaviour $B_C$ with behaviour properties $P_b$
(action, speed, height, width) and environment properties $P_e$ (stair height,
tunnel height, gap width, ground roughness); perception classifies the
environment and the planner returns entries satisfying every required property.
Daudelin et al.\ \cite{daudelin2018} instantiate this with four environment
classes --- free, tunnel, stairs, high --- derived from a 3D occupancy grid.
Two consequences follow for this subsystem. First, the reachability filter
below must be applied at query time, so that configurations containing cycles
are never returned. Second, the library query in the source work is blind to
reconfiguration cost, while the assignment stage below computes exactly that;
exposing that cost as a further selection criterion is an available extension
where two library entries would otherwise both satisfy the query.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Reachability filter.} With lateral docking implemented, the
tree-only assumption is the sole remaining restriction on the published
configuration set.

\begin{pblock}
\begin{longtable}{@{}L{0.28\textwidth} L{0.20\textwidth} l L{0.30\textwidth}@{}}

\textbf{Assembly requires} \\

\endhead

  TOP--BOTTOM actions only \\

\pending{%
\noindent Only cycles are unreachable. The practical constraint is therefore
not topology but module budget: every lateral edge consumes a helper module for
the duration of its action, so a configuration with several lateral edges at
the same depth serialises where a chain would not.
}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Task assignment.} The target tree is unfolded onto the plane
and its graph centre selected as the target root $g_\tau$ by a linear-time
root-module search; every target module is expressed as a relative pose
$[x, y, \theta]$ with respect to $g_\tau$. On the physical side the module
nearest the group centroid,
\begin{equation}
  m_\tau \;=\; \arg\min_{m_i \in M} \left\| o_i - o_c \right\|,
\end{equation}
is taken as the physical root, and every module expressed relative to it. The
cost matrix is $C_{ij} = \left\| \tilde{o}_i - \bar{o}_j \right\|$, and
Kuhn--Munkres returns the bijection $f : V \rightarrow M$ minimising total
travel. Complexity is $O(n^3)$ --- sub-millisecond at $n \leq 6$, confirming
that the algorithm earns its place by minimising physical motion rather than
computation.

\noindent\textbf{Proposed extension.} The inherited cost is orientation-blind.
We will evaluate
\begin{equation}
  C_{ij} \;=\; \left\| \tilde{o}_i - \bar{o}_j \right\|
              \;+\; \lambda \left| \Delta\theta \right|,
\end{equation}
penalising net reorientation, since in-place turning on a differential-drive
base is slower and less repeatable than translation; $\lambda$ is calibrated
from measured turn-versus-translate timing.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Assembly sequencing.} At depth $d$, each target module $v$
maps to $m = f(v)$ and yields an action $(m, c, \tilde{m}, \tilde{c}')$:
connect $m$'s connector $c$ to parent $\tilde{m}$'s connector $\tilde{c}'$.
Same-depth actions are mutually independent and form one action group. One
exception applies at the root: because it is unfixed and can be displaced,
actions on the root's LEFT/RIGHT faces precede those on its TOP/BOTTOM faces.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Navigation} \cite{binder2019,binder2017}\textbf{.} The
environment is a graph $G(V, E)$ whose vertices are path segments carrying a
length (shortest-route search) and a width (passability); in our case the
10\,cm grid of Liu et al.\ supplies this graph directly and width reduces to a
binary footprint test, so we inherit the algorithm without its map-building
stage. Each module is a differential-drive vehicle,
\begin{equation}
  \dot{p} = \begin{bmatrix} \dot{x} \\ \dot{y} \\ \dot{\theta} \end{bmatrix}
          = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 1
            \end{bmatrix}
            \begin{bmatrix} v \\ \omega \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
with $v$ and $\omega$ from the LEFT and RIGHT wheel DoFs. Modules are planned
one at a time in priority order by the Single Robot Route Planner, an A$^*$
search over $G$ that updates a vertex potential only if the vertex is
unoccupied at that time step, querying a Route Coordinator holding the
already-planned routes. Routes are stored as timed vertex locks, which permits
explicit waiting, permits a module to hold several vertices at a crossing, and
--- because locks overlap slightly in time --- makes a position swap register as
a collision rather than passing undetected.

On reaching an occupied vertex the Multi Robot Collision Resolver copies the
affected vertices into a temporary subgraph, leaving the original potentials
intact, and tries in order: backtracking (retreat to the first vertex not
shared with the blocker and wait), avoid-at-crossing (pull into a side branch),
avoid-at-start and avoid-at-goal (for modules that cannot back up or are
blocking from their goal), and push (displace the waiting vertex recursively
when no branch is free). If routing still fails, speed rescheduling lowers the
maximum speed of the module colliding most often with the failing one, and then
priority rescheduling swaps the priorities of the two modules with the most
unresolved collisions --- selecting by collision count rather than at random
avoids swaps between modules not actually in conflict. The Route Generator then
emits the routing table with its per-segment preconditions, and each module
follows its route under a PID path-following controller, publishing its segment
index so peers can evaluate preconditions; segments overlapping at a crossing
are locked together. A reference implementation exists as the ROS package
\texttt{tuw\_multi\_robot\_router}, which we will evaluate against
reimplementing on our stack.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Pose adjustment.} With pose error
$(x'_i, y'_i, \theta'_i)$ in the parent's frame, TOP/BOTTOM connections drive
$y'_i, \theta'_i \rightarrow 0$ and relax $x'_i$; LEFT/RIGHT connections drive
$x'_i, \theta'_i \rightarrow 0$ and relax $y'_i$. For the TOP/BOTTOM case,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{bmatrix} v \\ \omega \end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix} \sin\theta'_i & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}^{-1}
    K \begin{bmatrix} -y'_i \\ -\theta'_i \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad K = \mathrm{diag}(2, 1),
\end{equation}
relaxing one dimension to obtain full actuation over the remaining two under a
nonholonomic base. The relaxed coordinate is unregulated and may diverge,
risking collision on approach; we bound this by gating the approach on
$|x'_i|$ staying inside a measured envelope and by capping pose-adjustment
duration.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Approach.} For a TOP/BOTTOM connection the module drives
straight at the target connector until the EP connectors engage, pushing the
parent into contact where necessary.

A LEFT/RIGHT connection cannot be closed this way, because a differential-drive
base cannot translate along its wheel axis into the mating pose. The action is
therefore executed with a \emph{helper module}: the helper docks to the child's
TOP face, lifts the child clear of the ground, carries it into alignment with
the parent's lateral connector, sets it down so the EP faces engage, then
releases its own TOP connection and withdraws. The helper is unavailable for
other actions throughout, so the sequencer treats a lateral action as consuming
two modules rather than one when composing an action group.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Module localisation.} Poses are required in a common frame by
task assignment, by the route planner and by the PID path follower. Phase~A
uses the arrangement of Daudelin et al.\ \cite{daudelin2018}: an RGB-D camera
running RTAB-MAP SLAM builds a 3D occupancy grid of the environment, while
AprilTag fiducials on each module are tracked by a downward-facing camera
covering the reconfiguration area, giving module pose during navigation and the
final approach. Docking tolerance is therefore set by fiducial tracking
accuracy rather than by odometry drift, which is what makes the capture-envelope
threshold in pose adjustment measurable rather than assumed.

\medskip

\noindent\textbf{Baseline and evaluation protocol.} Each configuration has a
scripted, open-loop gait in the manner of PolyBot's table-driven gaits
\cite{yim2003}. Both controllers are evaluated over the same trial count on the
same obstacle, reporting traversal success rate and traversal time; a learned
policy is adopted only where it beats the scripted gait on both, in simulation
and then on hardware. The scripted gait remains the deployed fallback where it
does not.

\gsection{Expected Project Outcome}

\pending{%
The outcome of this project is a small fleet of prototype modules --- four to
six, the count itself an output of the actuator sizing of
Section~\ref{sec:theory} rather than an input --- together with a
physics-validated twin and the measured data supporting both. What the hardware
is meant to show is not a deployable system but a demonstrated level of
task-oriented behaviour: presented with an obstacle it was not given in
advance, the assembled robot estimates the parameter that matters, selects a
configuration to suit it, reconfigures, and traverses. That loop is closed on
one obstacle class in hardware; the claim that selection generalises beyond its
calibration set is evaluated in simulation, at parameter values held out of the
fit.

The scope is deliberately bounded. A prototype fleet of this size cannot
establish reliability figures, and a terrestrial testbed cannot qualify
anything for flight. What it can do is show that the decision layer works end
to end on real hardware at small scale, and measure how far it degrades when
the obstacle is one nobody specified in advance. Each deliverable below is
stated with the standard it has to meet, so that success is decidable rather
than a matter of opinion at the review.
}

\pending{%
\noindent\textbf{On the selection target.} The 80\,\% figure is measured
end-to-end from the \textit{estimated} obstacle parameter, not from a
ground-truth value. This matters, because the decision itself is small --- three
obstacle classes with one governing parameter each, resolving to three or four
configurations, and therefore a handful of thresholds that the torque analysis
of Section~\ref{sec:theory} largely determines in advance. Evaluated on exact
parameters the decision rule would be expected to score far above 80\,\%. The
residual is dominated by estimation error from a monocular camera and an
$8\times8$ time-of-flight grid with no prior map, which is where the difficulty
in this subsystem actually lies.

\noindent Success is counted as \textbf{the selected configuration traversing
the obstacle}, not as agreement with a labelled answer. Where two
configurations both succeed at a given parameter value, either choice is
correct; scoring against a label would count a working choice as a failure, and
would penalise the system hardest exactly where the ambiguity is genuine.

\noindent\textbf{On the comparison to \cite{liub2025}.} That result is for
modular self-reconfigurable satellites performing path planning. The bridge to
our target is module count and learning approach, not task domain --- no closer
learned-selection result appears to exist for terrestrial modular hardware. The
transfer is stated here rather than left for the reader to notice.

\noindent\textbf{On trial counts.} Twenty trials for docking is a deliberate
doubling of the ten used in \cite{an2026}. Twenty trials distinguish a working
connector from a marginal one; they do not resolve a few points of difference
in success rate, and no claim of that precision is made. Targets are stated as
counts rather than percentages so that the sample size stays visible.

\noindent\textbf{On residual failure.} Setting any bar at 100\,\% would not be
honest. Daudelin et al.\ categorised 24 recorded failures of a SMORES-EP
demonstration and attributed 10 of them (41.7\,\%) to hardware faults and
3 (12.5\,\%) to navigation \cite{daudelin2018} --- causes no algorithm removes.
A residual failure rate is a property of this class of system rather than a
shortfall in this instance of it.
}

\gsection{Summary and Benefit to Real Industry}

\pending{%
This project builds a modular self-reconfigurable robot that chooses its own
shape. Existing systems are told which configuration to form and then work out
how to form it; ours perceives an obstacle, infers what capability is needed to
pass it, selects a configuration, reconfigures, and traverses --- using only
the sensing and computation it carries. The work is staged over two semesters,
from a single module driving under external supervision to a cluster that
closes the whole loop on board, and is evaluated against three obstacle classes
with targets drawn from published results for comparable systems.

The contribution is the selection layer and the evidence that it survives
contact with hardware. Learning-based reconfiguration is almost entirely a
simulation literature, and contact dynamics and magnetic docking are exactly
what fails to transfer out of simulation. Building the twin against measured
values, and then reporting where the twin and the hardware disagree, is as much
a result as the traversal statistics.
}

\pending{\gsubsection{Benefit to real industry}}

\pending{%
\noindent\textbf{Space and planetary operations.} Agencies are already funding
modular reconfigurable hardware for on-orbit servicing and lunar surface work
\cite{an2026,mishra2025,liub2025}. The constraint those programmes run into is
the same one here: a robot several light-minutes away cannot be told which
shape to adopt. Any progress on autonomous configuration selection transfers
directly, and the terrestrial analogue arena is the standard way such systems
are qualified before flight.

\noindent\textbf{Search, rescue and disaster response.} Collapsed structures
present openings that no fixed-shape robot can pass, and the environment cannot
be surveyed beforehand. A cluster that can split, pass a gap one module at a
time, and reassemble addresses a capability gap that current USAR robots do not
close.

\noindent\textbf{Inspection of confined infrastructure.} Pipes, ducts, tanks
and cable runs share a shape: a small opening leading to a large interior.
Inspection robots today are built per-geometry. A modular system that
reconfigures to the geometry it finds replaces a family of single-purpose tools
with one inventory of identical units --- which also changes the spare-parts
economics, since every module is a spare for every other.

\noindent\textbf{Manufacturing.} Reconfigurable fixturing and small-batch
automation face the same argument in miniature: retooling is expensive because
tooling is specific. This is the least developed of the four directions and we
claim it only as an adjacency, not as a target application.

\medskip
\noindent More immediately, the project produces something the department keeps:
a documented, reproducible modular robot platform at a cost that a student team
can absorb. Most MSRR hardware is either a national-agency investment or a
fragile laboratory one-off, and the gap between those is where teaching and
follow-on research actually happen.
}

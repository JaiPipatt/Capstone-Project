\subsection{Design Decisions: Wheel Gear, Pan/Tilt Crown Gear, and Pinions}
\label{sec:gear-design}

\subsubsection{Starting Point: Reverse-Engineering the SMORES-EP Reference}

SMORES-EP is not open-source, so no CAD or drawing set was available. The gear
train therefore had to be reconstructed from published dimensions and
photographs and then re-derived from first principles.

The known reference parameters are listed in Table~\ref{tab:smores-ref}.

\begin{table}[htbp]
	\centering
	\caption{Reconstructed SMORES-EP reference parameters.}
	\label{tab:smores-ref}
	\begin{tabular}{ll}
		\toprule
		\textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{SMORES-EP} \\
		\midrule
		Module envelope & $80 \times 80 \times 80$\,mm \\
		Spur gear       & 48 teeth \\
		Crown gear      & Same part as the spur gear, used at \SI{90}{\degree} \\
		Pinion          & 9 teeth \\
		Gearbox         & 298:1 \\
		\bottomrule
	\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Assuming the \SI{80}{\milli\metre} cube is the full envelope (wheels included),
the largest gear that can physically fit is approximately \SI{70}{\milli\metre}
in outside diameter. Using the outside-diameter relation
\begin{equation}
	D_o = m(z + 2),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	m = \frac{70}{48 + 2} = 1.4 .
\end{equation}

This gives a consistent, self-checking picture of the original: module
\num{1.4}, pitch diameter \SI{67.2}{\milli\metre}, and a stage ratio of
$48/9 = 5.33$. This reconstructed module is the baseline against which all
scaling decisions below are made.

\subsubsection{Envelope Change and Its Consequences}

The new module is $100 \times 100 \times 100$\,mm rather than
\SI{80}{\milli\metre}. This was driven by internal packaging (motors,
gearboxes, electronics, and the pan/tilt joint) rather than by the gears
themselves, but it directly sets the available gear space.

Allowing for wall thickness, bearing seats, and clearance to the housing, the
largest practical spur gear outside diameter is \SI{90}{\milli\metre}.
Everything downstream follows from that number.

\subsubsection{Wheel Spur Gear and Pinion}

\paragraph{Tooth counts --- why the ratio was held constant.}
The decision made first, before any dimension was fixed, was to preserve the
original \num{5.33}:1 stage ratio:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Spur gear: \textbf{64 teeth}
	\item Pinion: \textbf{12 teeth}
	\item Ratio: $64/12 = 5.33$ --- identical to $48/9$
\end{itemize}

The ratio was prioritised over any other parameter for three reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{The gearbox selection stays valid.} The 298:1 gearbox
	      reduction combines with the external stage to give the same total
	      reduction as the reference design. Output speed and stall torque at
	      the wheel therefore remain comparable, so motor and gearbox sizing
	      carried over from SMORES-EP remains a defensible starting point
	      instead of an unverified guess.
	\item \textbf{Published SMORES-EP performance data remains a usable
	      benchmark.} Locomotion speed, climbing ability, and joint torque
	      figures reported for the original can be treated as an approximate
	      expectation for this design. If the ratio had been changed, every
	      performance claim would have had to be re-derived from scratch with
	      no reference to check against.
	\item \textbf{Control and firmware behaviour is preserved.} Encoder counts
	      per wheel revolution, velocity limits, and any feed-forward torque
	      terms scale with the total reduction. Holding it constant removes a
	      whole class of retuning work.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Why 64/12 rather than 48/9 scaled directly.}
Multiple tooth-count pairs give \num{5.33}:1. The pair 64/12 was chosen because
increasing the pinion from 9 to 12 teeth materially improves the pinion, which
is always the weakest and most heavily loaded element in the stage:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Reduced undercutting.} At a \SI{20}{\degree} pressure angle
	      the theoretical minimum tooth count before undercut is
	      $z_{\min} = 2/\sin^{2}\SI{20}{\degree} \approx 17$. A 9-tooth pinion
	      is far below this and suffers severe undercut at the tooth root,
	      removing material exactly where bending stress is highest. A 12-tooth
	      pinion is still below the theoretical limit, but the root section is
	      considerably healthier.
	\item \textbf{Higher contact ratio.} More teeth on the pinion increases the
	      length of the line of action relative to the base pitch, so more teeth
	      share the load at any instant. This reduces peak tooth stress and
	      smooths torque transmission.
	\item \textbf{Better load sharing per cycle.} A 12-tooth pinion completes
	      fewer revolutions per gear revolution than a 9-tooth pinion, so each
	      pinion tooth accumulates fewer loading cycles over the robot's life
	      --- a direct fatigue-life benefit for the most stressed part.
	\item \textbf{Larger pinion bore.} A 12-tooth pinion at this module has
	      enough body to carry the motor shaft bore and the wall thickness
	      around it without the bore breaking into the tooth roots.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Undercut outcome at 12 teeth.}
Because 12 teeth sits below the \num{17}-tooth theoretical threshold, some root
undercut is expected in the generated profile. On inspection of the generated
geometry and on assembling the mesh in CAD, the undercut present at 12 teeth
\textbf{does not cause interference}: the mating spur gear tooth tips clear the
undercut region throughout the mesh cycle at the design centre distance, and no
tip-to-root fouling is observed. The undercut is therefore accepted as a minor
reduction in root section rather than treated as a defect requiring correction,
and no profile shift was applied. This keeps the pinion a standard,
directly generated part.

\paragraph{Resulting geometry.}
With 64 teeth at a \SI{90}{\milli\metre} outside diameter,
\begin{equation}
	m = \frac{90}{64 + 2} = 1.3636 \approx 1.36 .
\end{equation}

The resulting geometry is summarised in Table~\ref{tab:spur-geom}.

\begin{table}[htbp]
	\centering
	\caption{Wheel spur gear and pinion geometry.}
	\label{tab:spur-geom}
	\begin{tabular}{ll}
		\toprule
		\textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{Value} \\
		\midrule
		Module, $m$                 & \num{1.36} \\
		Pressure angle              & \SI{20}{\degree} (standard) \\
		Spur gear teeth             & 64 \\
		Spur pitch diameter         & \SI{87.04}{\milli\metre} \\
		Spur outside diameter       & \SI{90}{\milli\metre} \\
		Pinion teeth                & 12 \\
		Pinion pitch diameter       & \SI{16.32}{\milli\metre} \\
		Pinion outside diameter     & \SI{19.04}{\milli\metre} \\
		Theoretical centre distance & \SI{51.68}{\milli\metre} \\
		Face width                  & \SI{5}{\milli\metre} \\
		Backlash                    & \SI{0.2}{\milli\metre} \\
		Root fillet                 & \SI{0.3}{\milli\metre} \\
		\bottomrule
	\end{tabular}
\end{table}

A \SI{20}{\degree} pressure angle was used throughout. It is the industry
standard, it gives thicker tooth roots and better load capacity than
\SI{14.5}{\degree}, it tolerates small centre-distance errors well (important
for a printed or lightly-toleranced housing), and it is the default assumption
of every standard gear generator and cutting tool, which keeps the design
manufacturable by more than one process.

The gears were generated using the \textbf{Fusion~360 spur gear extension},
entering pressure angle, module, tooth count, backlash, root fillet, and face
width directly. Using a parametric generator rather than hand-sketched profiles
guarantees a true involute flank and makes it cheap to regenerate the part when
an upstream parameter changes --- which, as the following subsection shows,
happened repeatedly.

The \SI{0.2}{\milli\metre} backlash and \SI{0.3}{\milli\metre} root fillet are
deliberate manufacturing allowances rather than theoretical values. Backlash
absorbs centre-distance error, shaft deflection, bearing play, and thermal
expansion, preventing tooth binding; the root fillet reduces the stress
concentration at the tooth root, which is the standard bending-failure
location.

\subsubsection{Pan/Tilt Crown Gear}

\paragraph{Departure from the SMORES-EP approach.}
SMORES-EP used a single gear part in two roles: as a conventional spur gear,
and as a de facto crown gear meshing at \SI{90}{\degree}. This is convenient for
manufacturing --- one part number, one tool --- but it is a compromise. A spur
profile engaged at \SI{90}{\degree} does not have conjugate contact with its
mate; contact degenerates toward a point or a short line near the tooth tip,
which concentrates stress, increases wear, and produces a mesh that is
sensitive to axial position.

This design instead uses a \textbf{true crown (face) gear}, with teeth formed on
the face of the disc and oriented vertically so that they mesh with the mating
spur gear. This gives line contact across the tooth face and a substantially
better load distribution for the same envelope.

\paragraph{Iteration history.}
The crown gear was the most constrained part in the assembly, because its
diameter had to coexist with the motors, the gearboxes, the two wheel gear
trains, and the structural frame --- all inside the \SI{100}{\milli\metre} cube.
The iterations are given in Table~\ref{tab:crown-iter}.

\begin{table}[htbp]
	\centering
	\caption{Crown gear sizing iterations.}
	\label{tab:crown-iter}
	\begin{tabular}{lllp{5.2cm}}
		\toprule
		\textbf{Iteration} & \textbf{Diameter} & \textbf{Teeth / Module} & \textbf{Outcome} \\
		\midrule
		1     & \SI{90}{\milli\metre} & 48 / \num{1.8}  & Interference with surrounding assembly --- rejected \\
		Final & \SI{84}{\milli\metre} & 46 / \num{1.75} & Fits with clearance \\
		\bottomrule
	\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The first attempt simply mirrored the spur gear's \SI{90}{\milli\metre}
diameter. On assembly it fouled adjacent components, so the diameter had to be
reduced. The final geometry is consistent with the same outside-diameter
relation:
\begin{equation}
	D_o = 1.75 \times (46 + 2) = \SI{84}{\milli\metre}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Module mismatch: an accepted, envelope-driven compromise.}
Standard gear practice requires that two meshing gears share the same module
and the same pressure angle, since the module fixes the circular pitch and
therefore whether the teeth index correctly against one another. That condition
could not be satisfied here. Two constraints fixed the geometry before the
module could be chosen freely:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Both diameters are fixed by packaging.} The spur gear outside
	      diameter is capped at \SI{90}{\milli\metre} by the
	      \SI{100}{\milli\metre} envelope, and the crown gear outside diameter
	      is capped at \SI{84}{\milli\metre} by interference with the motors,
	      gearboxes, and frame --- as established in the iteration above. Neither
	      diameter is a free variable.
	\item \textbf{The tooth counts are fixed by the ratios being preserved.} The
	      spur gear must have 64 teeth to hold the \num{5.33}:1 wheel stage
	      ratio, and the crown gear must stay near 46--48 teeth to hold the
	      near-1:1 spur-to-crown relationship of the original design.
\end{enumerate}

With both $D_o$ and $z$ fixed on each part, $m = D_o/(z+2)$ is fully determined,
and the two values simply are not equal: \num{1.36} for the spur gear against
\num{1.75} for the crown gear. Forcing a common module would have required
either abandoning the preserved gear ratios --- discarding the benchmark value
of the SMORES-EP reference data and the validity of the motor and gearbox
selection --- or enlarging a gear beyond the envelope it has to fit in.

The design therefore treats matched modules as the parameter to sacrifice,
because it is the one whose relaxation is recoverable at assembly (through
tooth-depth and backlash adjustment) rather than structural. Concretely, the
crown tooth depth was tuned during iteration to obtain a working mesh at the
assembled centre distance, and the dedendum modification described below is
part of that adjustment. This is a documented deviation from standard practice
rather than an oversight, and it is one of the primary items to be validated
once physical parts exist.

\paragraph{Why tooth count was held near 1:1 with the spur gear.}
The original design has an effective 1:1 tooth-count relationship between spur
and crown (48 and 48). Rather than abandoning this when the diameter had to
shrink, the design keeps the counts as close as the envelope permits:
\textbf{46 teeth against the original's 48}, a \SI{4}{\percent} deviation.

Keeping the tooth counts nearly equal, and therefore the tooth size nearly
equal, matters for several reasons:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{The pan/tilt joint ratio stays near unity.} With a near-1:1
	      mesh, output speed and torque at the joint are close to what the
	      driving stage delivers. The joint's angular velocity and holding
	      torque are therefore predictable from the motor and gearbox
	      specification without an extra reduction factor to characterise, and
	      the SMORES-EP joint performance data stays usable as a benchmark.
	\item \textbf{Tooth size --- not just ratio --- sets load capacity.} Tooth
	      bending strength scales roughly with the square of the module for a
	      given face width and material (Lewis bending equation). A 46-tooth
	      crown at $m = 1.75$ has \emph{larger} teeth than the 64-tooth spur at
	      $m = 1.36$, so the crown teeth are individually stronger. This is
	      desirable: the crown gear carries the full pan/tilt reaction load,
	      including any static holding torque when the joint is supporting the
	      robot's own weight or resisting an external load, and it is the harder
	      part to replace.
	\item \textbf{Mesh symmetry between the two driving gears.} Because two
	      identical spur gears drive the single crown from opposite sides,
	      keeping the crown's tooth size and count close to symmetric ensures
	      both meshes see comparable loading and wear, rather than one side
	      dominating.
	\item \textbf{No hidden speed asymmetry between pan and tilt.} Pan and tilt
	      are produced by the same two inputs in common mode or differential
	      mode. A near-1:1 crown keeps the effective ratio for both modes
	      similar, so the two motions have comparable speed and torque
	      characteristics and can share a single control tuning.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Dedendum modification.}
Standard practice sets the dedendum to $1.25\,m$, which for $m = 1.75$ gives
\SI{2.1875}{\milli\metre}. The design uses a slightly reduced
\SI{2.15}{\milli\metre}.

This small reduction (\SI{0.0375}{\milli\metre}, about \SI{1.7}{\percent}) trims
the root depth just enough to clear the mating tooth tip within the assembled
centre distance without carving further into the root section. Because tooth
bending strength is governed by the root thickness, a shallower dedendum
marginally \emph{increases} the root section and therefore does not weaken the
tooth --- the trade-off is a small reduction in tip clearance, which is
acceptable given the \SI{0.2}{\milli\metre} backlash already designed in. It is
a compromise between manufacturing feasibility and the clearance required to
avoid tip-to-root interference.

\subsubsection{Pinion Mounting and Tolerances}

The two pinion variants differ only in how they attach to the motor shaft,
which is dictated by their position in the assembly
(Table~\ref{tab:pinion-mount}).

\begin{table}[htbp]
	\centering
	\caption{Pinion bore variants.}
	\label{tab:pinion-mount}
	\begin{tabular}{llp{6.2cm}}
		\toprule
		\textbf{Pinion} & \textbf{Bore} & \textbf{Rationale} \\
		\midrule
		Pan/tilt & Through-hole &
		The motor shaft passes fully through the pinion, allowing the shaft to be
		supported on both sides and the pinion to sit at an arbitrary axial
		position along it \\
		\addlinespace
		Wheel & Blind hole &
		Fitted onto the end of the shaft; the closed face provides a positive
		axial stop and a sealed, stiffer end \\
		\bottomrule
	\end{tabular}
\end{table}

A \SI{0.1}{\milli\metre} tolerance is applied to the shaft bores. This is a
clearance/transition allowance covering manufacturing variation in both the
printed or machined bore and the shaft diameter. Too tight and the pinion cracks
or cannot be assembled; too loose and backlash appears at the shaft interface,
degrading positional accuracy and introducing shock loading on direction
reversal.

The spur gear carries a bolt-hole pattern on its web for attachment to the wheel
hub and to adjacent structural parts. Placing the holes in the web rather than
near the rim keeps them clear of the tooth root region, where bending stress is
highest.

\subsubsection{Drivetrain Architecture}

The module uses \textbf{four motors driving four spur gears}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Outer two spur gears} --- one per side, each driving a wheel
	      directly. These provide differential-drive locomotion.
	\item \textbf{Inner two spur gears} --- both mesh with the \textbf{single
	      crown gear} that carries the two side-mounted spur gears.
\end{itemize}

The pan/tilt degrees of freedom are produced differentially from these two
inner inputs:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Inner gears rotating in the \textbf{same direction} (common mode)
	      $\rightarrow$ \textbf{pan}
	\item Inner gears rotating in \textbf{opposite directions} (differential
	      mode) $\rightarrow$ \textbf{tilt}
\end{itemize}

This differential arrangement is the main structural reason the crown gear
geometry matters so much. Both inner spur gears must mesh with the same crown
gear simultaneously and symmetrically; any asymmetry in the two meshes appears
as a coupling error between the pan and tilt axes, not merely as local wear. It
also explains why crown gear diameter was the binding constraint during
iteration --- it must remain concentric with the joint axis while two separate
drive trains and two wheel trains are packed around it.

\subsubsection{Current Status and Remaining Work}

All of the geometry above exists as a CAD model only. \textbf{No part of the
gear train has been 3D printed, manufactured, or tested in the real world at the
time of writing.} Every clearance, interference, and mesh result reported in
this section was obtained from the CAD assembly, not from physical hardware, and
should be read as a design intent rather than a verified outcome.

The following items are therefore outstanding and are the priority for the next
phase:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Print a first article of the spur gear, both pinions, and the crown
	      gear, and verify that the spur/crown mesh functions despite the
	      module mismatch discussed above.
	\item Confirm that the 12-tooth pinion's undercut remains non-interfering at
	      real printed tolerances, where layer resolution and shrinkage are
	      additional error sources not captured in CAD.
	\item Check that the \SI{0.2}{\milli\metre} backlash and
	      \SI{0.1}{\milli\metre} bore tolerance are appropriate for the chosen
	      printing process and material; adjust and reprint if the fit is tight
	      or loose.
	\item Measure the achieved pan and tilt ranges and the joint holding torque
	      against the SMORES-EP benchmark figures used to justify the ratio
	      decisions.
	\item Run an endurance check on the crown gear teeth, which carry the
	      highest sustained load in the assembly.
\end{itemize}
